# Burnham bounce reverses Reform's 2026 polling lead

Britain's 2026 polling tells one story in two halves: Reform UK began the year leading every Westminster poll by roughly ten points (about 29% to Labour's 19%) and ended it in third place with several pollsters (20–25%), while Labour, which averaged 18% in the first half of the year and lost more than 1,400 councillors, Wales and its second place in Scotland on 7 May, climbed to 26–32% within three months of Andy Burnham replacing Keir Starmer as Prime Minister on 20 July ([YouGov, 3 Nov 2026](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55883-voting-intention-1-2-november-2026-lab-26-con-24-ref-21-ld-11-grn-10); [Ipsos, 22 Oct 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/en-uk/labour-now-lead-8-points-conservatives-second-place-latest-voting-intention-polling)). The Conservatives under Kemi Badenoch recovered to 20–24% and second place with YouGov, Ipsos, More in Common and Find Out Now; the Greens, who hit a record 21% in March after winning the Gorton and Denton by-election, fell back to 8–11% as Zack Polanski's ratings collapsed to −37; and Rupert Lowe's Restore Britain, registered on 20 March, took 3–8% largely from Reform's right flank. The qualitative record (more than 40 More in Common focus groups around the May elections, 11 in Makerfield, groups in Clacton, Holborn and the Chancellor's and Energy Secretary's seats, plus eight Lord Ashcroft rounds) shows why: voters describe a broken social contract ("I've never earned as much money in my work, but I've never felt as poor"), a Starmer government that "stole the name" of Labour, a Reform vote cast "in spite of Nigel Farage, not because of him", and a Burnham honeymoon that is personal, conditional and already being tested by a Budget 60% call "tinkering round the edges" ([More in Common, Makerfield](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Makerfield-Report-typeset.pdf); [More in Common, Post-Budget Debrief](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/research/more-in-commons-post-budget-debrief/)). For a progressive campaign-tech practitioner the strategically decisive findings are that Labour's recovery came almost entirely from returning Green, Lib Dem and undecided voters rather than from Reform switchers (only 11% of Labour-to-Reform defectors would consider Labour; 52% score Labour 0/10), that anti-Reform tactical voting is now strong enough to cost Reform 20–85 seats depending on the model, and that welfare "fairness to the squeezed middle", not immigration, had become the government's decisive credibility test by autumn ([YouGov Big Voter Study](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55153-big-yougov-voter-study-2026-how-solid-is-reform-uks-rise); [Electoral Calculus tactical voting](https://www.electoralcalculus.co.uk/blogs/pseph_tactical_2026.html)). All of this rests on a local archive of roughly 480 MB in seven folders under `research_notes/UK polling and focus groups 2026/data/`, inventoried in the penultimate section, which holds every 2026 Westminster poll, every Holyrood, Senedd and NI poll, 939 leader-rating rows, 1,022 issue-tracker rows, 688 demographic cross-break rows, all six by-election results at candidate level, 28 More in Common qualitative reports and 44 More in Common, 31 Opinium, 22 Ipsos and 29 YouGov data tables; where earlier snippet-based figures conflict with these primary tables (Britpolls' local-election tallies, Starmer's "final" YouGov rating, the October Ipsos Issues Index), this report uses the primary-sourced figure and says so.

## Four acts: Reform's winter lead, May's rout, Starmer's exit, Burnham's autumn

The year opened with Labour in free fall and Reform looking like a government-in-waiting. In January four Conservative MPs (Robert Jenrick, Suella Braverman, Nadhim Zahawi and Andrew Rosindell) defected to Reform, the Standards Commissioner found Farage had breached rules 17 times over £384,000 of undeclared income, and Starmer's YouGov net favourability fell to **−57** (75% unfavourable), equal to Rishi Sunak's June 2024 low with only Liz Truss worse ([Wikipedia, 2026 in UK politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2026_in_United_Kingdom_politics_and_government&action=raw); [YouGov, Jan 2026](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/53907-political-favourability-ratings-january-2026)). Ipsos recorded Starmer at 15% satisfied, 77% dissatisfied — "no Prime Minister going back to 1979 has ever recorded a figure this low at this stage" ([Ipsos, Jan 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/en-uk/reform-uk-still-holds-8-point-lead-gap-shrinks-amid-doubts-over-their-readiness-government-ipsos)). February brought the Mandelson–Epstein scandal, Morgan McSweeney's resignation as chief of staff, and on **26 February the Greens' first-ever by-election win** when plumber Hannah Spencer took Gorton and Denton with 40.7%, Reform's Matt Goodwin second on 28.7% and Labour third on 25.4% — the seventh-largest Labour majority ever overturned at a by-election and the first Conservative lost deposit since 1962 ([Members API result 554](https://members-api.parliament.uk/api/Location/Constituency/4066/ElectionResult/554); [Wikipedia, Gorton and Denton by-election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Gorton_and_Denton_by-election)). The Green surge that followed took the party to **21% with YouGov on 1–2 March**, ahead of Labour and the Conservatives for the first time, and to a record 15.8% monthly average in March ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-03/uk-greens-pass-labour-and-tories-for-first-time-in-yougov-poll); [ERS, March 2026](https://electoral-reform.org.uk/polling-breakdown-from-march-2026-latest-polls-see-continued-fragmentation/)). March also brought the US–Israeli war with Iran, which doubled public concern about defence, and on **20 March Rupert Lowe registered Restore Britain** as a party ([Ipsos Issues Index, Mar 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/news/documents/2026-03/Ipsos%20Issues%20Index_Mar26.pdf); [Wikipedia, Opinion polling for the next UK general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opinion_polling_for_the_next_United_Kingdom_general_election)).

The 7 May elections were the hinge. In England Reform won **1,454 of 5,066 councillors (+1,452) and 14 councils**, Labour fell to 1,068 (−1,498) and lost control of 38 councils, and the BBC's Projected National Share was Reform 26, Green 18, Labour 17, Conservative 17, Lib Dem 16 ([Wikipedia, 2026 UK local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_Kingdom_local_elections)). In Wales Plaid Cymru won the first election under the 96-seat closed-list system with 43 seats on 35.4%, Reform took 34 seats (29.3%) and Labour collapsed to 9 seats (11.1%), with First Minister Eluned Morgan losing her own seat and Rhun ap Iorwerth becoming the first non-Labour First Minister on 12 May ([Wikipedia, 2026 Senedd election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Senedd_election); [Senedd Record, 12 May 2026](https://www.theyworkforyou.com/wales/?id=2026-05-12.3.762202.h)). In Scotland the SNP won 58 of 129 seats, seven short of a majority, with Reform and Labour tied on 17, the Greens 15, Conservatives 12 and Lib Dems 10, and John Swinney was re-elected First Minister on 19 May in a three-round vote on 56 of 105 votes cast ([Wikipedia, 2026 Scottish Parliament election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Scottish_Parliament_election); [Scottish Parliament Official Report, 19 May 2026](https://www.theyworkforyou.com/sp/?id=2026-05-19.8.0)). Within a week 72 Labour MPs had publicly urged Starmer to go or set a timetable, Wes Streeting resigned from Cabinet on 14 May, and the same day Josh Simons resigned as MP for Makerfield to make way for Burnham ([Wikipedia, 2026 UK local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_Kingdom_local_elections)).

Burnham's return reshaped the year. He won **Makerfield on 18 June with 54.8%** (Reform's Robert Kenyon 34.5%, Restore Britain's Rebecca Shepherd 6.8%) on a 58.7% turnout — higher than the 2024 general election in the seat, the first time that had happened since Greenwich in 1987, and the first swing to a governing party at a by-election since Hartlepool 2021 ([Members API result 558](https://members-api.parliament.uk/api/Location/Constituency/4165/ElectionResult/558); [Wikipedia, Makerfield by-election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Makerfield_by-election)). The same day the Conservatives gained Aberdeen South from the SNP and the SNP held Arbroath and Broughty Ferry, both vacated by SNP MPs who had won Holyrood seats. Starmer announced his resignation on **22 June**; Burnham was the only validly nominated candidate (349 of 403 Labour MPs per Wikipedia; LabourList says all but 24), was confirmed leader at a special conference on 17 July without a members' ballot and became Prime Minister on **20 July**, the fifth change of PM since 2019 ([Wikipedia, 2026 Labour leadership crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Labour_Party_leadership_crisis); [LabourList](https://labourlist.org/2026/07/andy-burnham-becomes-new-labour-leader/); [Members API, Burnham](https://members-api.parliament.uk/api/Members/1427/Biography)). His cabinet put John Healey in the Treasury, Louise Haigh as First Secretary, Ed Miliband at the Foreign Office, Shabana Mahmood at the Home Office and Streeting at Defence ([Members API, GovernmentPosts](https://members-api.parliament.uk/api/Posts/GovernmentPosts)). Farage, under parliamentary scrutiny over a £5m gift from Christopher Harborne and other undeclared support, vacated Clacton on 7 July to seek a fresh mandate and was re-elected on **13 August with 63.3%** against a record 34-candidate field boycotted by Labour, the Conservatives, Lib Dems, Greens and Restore, with Count Binface's 26.9% thought to be the highest ever for a novelty candidate ([Wikipedia, 2026 Clacton by-election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Clacton_by-election); [Members API result 559](https://members-api.parliament.uk/api/Location/Constituency/3989/ElectionResult/559)). September brought Channel 4's undercover film of Farage celebrating foreign donations, a Met investigation, £36m donations each from Ben Delo and Harborne, and Burnham's conference pledges of a National Commission on Electoral Reform, a manifesto commitment to change the voting system, a national care service and replacing the triple lock with a double lock by 2030 ([Wikipedia, 2026 in UK politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2026_in_United_Kingdom_politics_and_government&action=raw); [ERS, 30 Sep 2026](https://electoral-reform.org.uk/looking-back-on-the-last-20-months-of-uk-political-polls/)). Labour then held Starmer's old seat of **Holborn and St Pancras on 8 October**, Sagal Abdi-Wali beating Polanski 45.2% to 32.8%, four days after the Green conference passed a "Zionism is racism" motion; the Board of Deputies declared the Greens a racist party on 25 October ([Members API result 560](https://members-api.parliament.uk/api/Location/Constituency/4105/ElectionResult/560)). Healey's first Budget on 28 October — gas standing charges abolished, a £100 energy payment to every household, higher tax on savings and property income — was the best-received fiscal event in three years but moved voting intention little ([Opinium, 28 Oct 2026](https://www.opinium.com/resource-center/voting-intention-28th-october-2026/); [YouGov Budget reaction](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55845-how-have-britons-reacted-to-the-2026-budget)). For reading any 2026 cross-tab, the year therefore divides into a Starmer phase (January–June) and a Burnham phase (late July onward); a January Reform-by-age figure does not describe November.

## 270 Westminster polls trace a reversal from Reform +10 to Labour +7

Twenty organisations published GB Westminster voting intention in 2026, producing 270 standard polls and 11 MRP rows in the Wikipedia table to 4 November. The weekly core is YouGov for The Times and Sky (47 polls), Find Out Now (47), More in Common (45) and Opinium for The Observer (29); Survation (15), Freshwater Strategy for City AM (11), Deltapoll (11), BMG for the i (10), Lord Ashcroft (9, flagged as a non-BPC member), Ipsos (8), Trajectory Partnership (8), JL Partners (7) and Techne (5, none after mid-May) are fortnightly to monthly, and Focaldata, Verian, Convergent Opinion and the Good Growth Foundation published one-offs ([Wikipedia polling table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opinion_polling_for_the_next_United_Kingdom_general_election)). Redfield & Wilton, Savanta, WeThink, Whitestone, Stack Data, Public First, Norstat and Merlin Strategy published no GB Westminster VI that Wikipedia recorded in 2026, so the BritPolls house-effects page that still lists them is stale. Three independent monthly series — the Electoral Reform Society's average of each BPC member's last poll of the month, Mark Pack's PollBase and simple means of the scraped Wikipedia table — agree on the shape of the year.

In April the ERS noted "no fewer than five parties separated by just 14.2 percentage points … a new record"; July was "the first time that a party other than Reform UK has topped our monthly polling average since March 2025"; by August Labour's 26.3% was its highest since the ERS series began and the Conservatives were above 20% for the first time since April 2025 ([ERS Apr](https://electoral-reform.org.uk/what-did-the-uk-polls-say-in-april-2026/); [ERS Jul](https://electoral-reform.org.uk/what-do-the-uk-political-polling-averages-for-july-2026-point-to/); [ERS Aug](https://electoral-reform.org.uk/august-2026-in-the-polls-the-latest-uk-monthly-political-polling-averages/)). The six inflection points are the late-February Green surge after Gorton and Denton, Restore Britain's registration on 20 March (pollsters that prompt for it show Reform one to three points lower), a small Reform rebound after 7 May, the Starmer–Burnham handover in late June and July (Labour +4 and Reform −2 in a single month), the continued Labour climb and Conservative recovery through October, and a well-received Budget that produced no immediate VI movement.

By early November every pollster had Labour first, between 26% (YouGov) and 32% (Ipsos, Deltapoll), with second place depending on the house: Opinium, Deltapoll, Survation and Freshwater had Reform, while YouGov, Ipsos, More in Common and Find Out Now had the Conservatives. YouGov's 1–2 November poll (Lab 26, Con 24, Ref 21, LD 11, Grn 10, Restore 3) recorded "the highest share recorded for the Conservatives since our voting intention tracker restarted in January 2025", a week after Reform's 20% was its lowest ([YouGov, 3 Nov 2026](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55883-voting-intention-1-2-november-2026-lab-26-con-24-ref-21-ld-11-grn-10); [YouGov, 25–26 Oct](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55822-voting-intention-25-26-october-2026-lab-27-con-22-ref-20-ld-11-grn-10)). Ipsos's 15–22 October poll had Labour 32 (+5), Conservatives 24 (+3), Reform 20 (−3), with the Conservatives losing 16% of their 2024 vote to Reform (22% a month earlier), Labour losing 5% to the Greens (12%), and Reform now losing 15% to the Conservatives; only 19% thought Reform ready to form a government ([Ipsos, 22 Oct 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/en-uk/labour-now-lead-8-points-conservatives-second-place-latest-voting-intention-polling)). Compared with each pollster's first 2026 poll, Reform is down 4–12 points everywhere, Labour up 7–13, the Conservatives up 0–5, the Greens down 2–7 and the Lib Dems down 1–5 ([Wikipedia polling table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opinion_polling_for_the_next_United_Kingdom_general_election)). Survation's Damian Lyons-Lowe summarised the market on 27 October: "Labour leads everywhere, by anything from 3 to 8 points … Pooled, Conservatives & Reform sit level on 22. Widest spreads are Labour (26–32) & Greens (7–13)" ([PollingReport UK](https://pollingreport.uk/articles/pollsters-house-effects/)).

### MRPs swung from a 381-seat Reform majority to a Labour majority of 56

Seat models tracked the same reversal with a lag. More in Common's January MRP (n>16,000) gave **Reform 381 seats (majority 112)** on 31%, Labour 85 and the Greens 9; Electoral Calculus's January PLMR MRP had Reform on 335 with Labour sixth ([MiC Jan MRP](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/latest-insights/more-in-common-s-january-mrp/); [EC Jan 2026](https://www.electoralcalculus.co.uk/blogs/ec_vipoll_20260113.html)). In April More in Common had Reform "one MP short of a majority" on 324, with 16 cabinet ministers losing seats, while Electoral Calculus — which applies a tactical-voting adjustment — had Reform down to 188 with the most likely outcome a Reform–Conservative coalition ([MiC Apr MRP](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/latest-insights/more-in-common-s-april-2026-mrp/); [EC Apr 2026](https://www.electoralcalculus.co.uk/blogs/ec_vipoll_20260423.html)). More in Common's "final MRP of Keir Starmer's premiership" (fieldwork 5–28 June) still had Reform largest on 293 with Labour 110 and "no seats at all in Wales"; Survation for Hope not Hate (22–27 July, n=10,014) had Reform 227 to Labour 197 ([MiC Jul MRP](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/research/the-final-mrp-of-keir-starmers-premiership/); [Survation](https://www.survation.com/first-past-the-post-but-only-just-reforms-marginal-lead-and-our-latest-mrp-findings/)). Then the September models flipped: **YouGov's first post-Burnham MRP** (23 Aug–18 Sep, n=16,758) projected Labour 241, Conservatives 130, Reform 123, Lib Dems 71, with 37% of seats decided by five points or less and 67% of Reform's projected seats marginal; More in Common (21 Aug–21 Sep, n=15,765) had Labour 250, Reform 147, Conservatives 125, with "102 of the 147 seats Reform UK is projected to win (69 per cent) … won by fewer than five points"; Survation for 38 Degrees had Labour 273; Electoral Calculus for Restore Britain had Labour 288 ([YouGov MRP](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55618-yougov-mrp-shows-labour-would-be-the-largest-party-in-a-hung-parliament-were-an-election-held-tomorrow); [MiC Sep MRP](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/research/more-in-commons-september-2026-mrp/); [EC Sep 2026](https://www.electoralcalculus.co.uk/blogs/ec_vipoll_20260924.html)). Electoral Calculus's October PLMR MRP (25 Sep–2 Oct, n=6,699) was the first to project a **Labour majority — 353 seats (majority 56)** on 28% to Reform 21% and Conservatives 18%, Reform's fall "partly due to the growth in support for Restore Britain", and its 27 October poll-of-polls gave Labour 365 seats and a majority of 80 with Reform fourth on 57 ([EC Oct 2026](https://www.electoralcalculus.co.uk/blogs/ec_vipoll_20261016.html); [EC prediction](https://www.electoralcalculus.co.uk/prediction_main.html)). The three MRP houses converge on Labour as largest party but differ on a majority, and the gap is driven by Labour's headline share (EC 28% vs 24–26% for YouGov and More in Common) rather than the modelling. YouGov's model still projected Labour losing 172 of its 411 seats, 102 to Reform, with Wales producing "Labour's worst Westminster result in the country since 1906", and in 82% of simulations Labour, the Lib Dems and the SNP held a majority between them ([YouGov MRP](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55618-yougov-mrp-shows-labour-would-be-the-largest-party-in-a-hung-parliament-were-an-election-held-tomorrow)).

### House effects: Restore prompting explains most of the Reform spread

Computed against the all-pollster monthly mean across 2026, Lord Ashcroft (−4.9) and YouGov (−1.6) run lowest for Reform and Deltapoll (+2.3), Freshwater (+2.0), JL Partners (+1.4) and More in Common (+1.1) highest; for the Greens, Ashcroft (+3.9), Verian (+3.7), Find Out Now (+3.1) and YouGov (+1.5) run high and Focaldata, Trajectory and More in Common (−2.8) low; Trajectory (+4.3), Deltapoll (+2.6) and Ipsos (+2.2) are Labour-high and Find Out Now (−2.6) and YouGov (−1.8) Labour-low ([derived from Wikipedia table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opinion_polling_for_the_next_United_Kingdom_general_election), file `data/wikipedia_2026_house_effects_vs_monthly_mean.csv`). The Reform-high firms are mostly those that do not prompt for Restore Britain (Deltapoll, Freshwater, Opinium, Survation, JL Partners), while Find Out Now/Electoral Calculus (prompting from June, Restore at 7–8%), More in Common (from its September MRP, "the first … to include Restore Britain as a prompted option"), YouGov, BMG and Trajectory show Reform one to three points lower — consistent with Electoral Calculus's and More in Common's statements that Restore splits the right ([EC Jul 2026](https://www.electoralcalculus.co.uk/blogs/ec_vipoll_20260708.html); [MiC Sep MRP](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/research/more-in-commons-september-2026-mrp/)). Ipsos's Political Monitor uses a random-probability KnowledgePanel introduced in June 2025 that prompts for Reform, so its comparisons with pre-2025 waves "need to be made with caution" ([Ipsos, 22 Oct 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/en-uk/labour-now-lead-8-points-conservatives-second-place-latest-voting-intention-polling)). No British Polling Council statement from 2026 could be retrieved; the BPC site was unreachable throughout.

## Burnham peaks at +13 while government approval lags and Farage hits record lows

Starmer left office with the worst ratings of any modern Prime Minister. Ipsos's data tables put him at 15% satisfied, 77% dissatisfied (**−62**) in January and 17/76 (−59) in its 25–30 June wave, "the lowest final rating for a PM at Ipsos before they left office (going back to 1979)"; government satisfaction was 13/79 (−66) in both January and May ([Ipsos tables, Jan 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/news/documents/2026-01/politmkp_w1jan2026web1.pdf); [Ipsos tables, Jun 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/news/documents/2026-07/politmkp_w6june2026web1.pdf); [Ipsos, record highs to resignation](https://www.ipsos.com/en-uk/record-highs-resignation)). The earlier snippet-based figure of −54 for January is superseded by the −62 in the primary table. A conflict in the earlier notes over Starmer's "final" YouGov rating is also resolved by the primary PDFs: **−45** (24% favourable, 69% unfavourable) is the 14–15 June wave, the last before his resignation announcement and repeated on 21–22 June, while **−36** (28/64) is the 12–13 July wave taken while he was caretaker PM, which is the figure YouGov's October article uses as his "final rating as prime minister"; both are correct for their dates ([YouGov Internal_Favourability_260615](https://ygo-assets-websites-editorial-emea.yougov.net/documents/Internal_Favourability_260615.pdf); [YouGov Copy_of_Internal_Favourability_260713](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/Copy_of_Internal_Favourability_260713.pdf)). The same July survey found 16% calling his premiership good or great and 51% poor or terrible ([YouGov Starmer retrospective](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/Internal_StarmerRetrospective_260713.pdf)).

Burnham's honeymoon was real, personal and front-loaded. YouGov's first reading of him as PM (21–22 July) was 38% favourable, 35% unfavourable, **+3**, "the most popular new PM since Theresa May" (Starmer's first was −3, Johnson −18, Sunak −19, Truss −31); he peaked at **+13** on 13–14 August (46/33), "the highest for any PM since May 2020", on favourable views from 76% of 2024 Labour voters, 68% of Lib Dems, 53% of Greens, 31% of Conservatives and 15% of Reform voters; he was +7 in September and **+4** by 15–16 October as unfavourables reached 42%, the highest of his premiership ([YouGov Jul](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55228-political-favourability-ratings-july-2026); [YouGov Aug](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55382-political-favourability-ratings-august-2026); [YouGov Oct](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55782-political-favourability-ratings-october-2026)). Opinium's approval series ran +16 (29 July) → +11 (12 August) → +4 (16 September) → +7 (28 October, "remains the most positively rated major party leader"); More in Common's weekly good-job/bad-job measure peaked at +20 (31 July–2 August) and settled at +10 to +16 through October (40/30 on 31 Oct–1 Nov); Deltapoll had him as high as +26 on 21–24 August ([Opinium 28 Oct tables](https://www.opinium.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/VI-2026-10-28-Opinium-Observer-Tables-Web-Tables-1403.xlsx); [MiC 31 Oct–1 Nov tables](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/31-10-to-01-11-Voting-Intention.xlsx); [Deltapoll Aug](https://deltapoll.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/260825-DeltapollVIetc.pdf)). Ipsos's harder "satisfied with job as PM" question is the outlier: 29% satisfied, 30% dissatisfied and 41% don't know in his first reading (30 July–4 August), "below Keir Starmer's first-month score (37%)", and 35/45 (−10) by 15–22 October ([Ipsos tables, Jul–Aug 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/news/documents/2026-08/Ipsos%20Political%20Monitor%20Tables%20July-August%202026.pdf); [Ipsos, 22 Oct 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/en-uk/labour-now-lead-8-points-conservatives-second-place-latest-voting-intention-polling)). On "best Prime Minister" he dominated every format: Ipsos most-capable-PM 36% to Farage 15 and Badenoch 13 (Jul/Aug), YouGov head-to-heads 52–22 over Farage and 42–26 over Badenoch (August), Ipsos preferred-government 52–29 for Burnham-led Labour over Farage-led Reform (October) ([Ipsos Aug](https://www.ipsos.com/en-uk/labour-leads-reform-uk-three-points); [YouGov best PM Aug](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55496-who-would-make-the-best-prime-minister-august-2026); [Ipsos Pulse Oct tables](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/news/documents/2026-10/Ipsos%20Political%20Pulse%20%28r1%29%20-%20October%202026%20-%20Tables.pdf)).

## Immigration tops concern all year but defence and inflation shocks defined spring

The Ipsos Issues Index charts PDFs for every month from January to September 2026 are in the archive, and they show immigration as the top unprompted concern in every month but one: **41% (January), 37 (March), 32 (April), 41 (May), 39 (June), 43 (July), 42 (August), 44 (September)**, with a dip in April when the economy edged ahead at 33% ([Ipsos Issues Index, Jan 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/news/documents/2026-02/Ipsos%20Issues%20Index_Jan26_v1_PUBLIC.pdf); [Sep 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/news/documents/2026-09/Ipsos%20Issues%20Index_Sept%2026_V1_IUO.pdf)). The movers were elsewhere: defence and foreign affairs doubled from 15% in February to **31% in March** after the Iran conflict began, "highest since the start of the Russian invasion of Ukraine in 2022", then fell back to 11% by August; inflation jumped seven points to **30% in April**, "highest since June 2024", as economic optimism hit **−72, the lowest since Ipsos began the series in 1978** (78% expecting the economy to worsen), eased to 21% in August and rose again to 27% in September ([Ipsos Issues Index, Mar 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/news/documents/2026-03/Ipsos%20Issues%20Index_Mar26.pdf); [Apr 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/news/documents/2026-04/Ipsos%20Issues%20Index_Apr26_v2_IUO.pdf)). The October 2026 Issues Index figures quoted in earlier notes (immigration 40, economy 31, inflation 30) came only from a search snippet; no October release exists on ipsos.com as of 6 November and the Ipsos topic page lists September as the latest, so those figures should be treated as unverified ([Ipsos Issues Index topic page](https://www.ipsos.com/en-uk/topic/issues-index)). Ipsos's October Political Monitor found Reform still best on asylum and immigration (28% vs Labour 14%, but down from 35% a year earlier), Labour now first on reducing the cost of living (21 vs Con 13, Reform 11 — Reform had led in November 2025) and healthcare (27 vs Con 11), and Labour and the Conservatives tied on the economy (20/18) and defence (18/19) ([Ipsos, 22 Oct 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/en-uk/labour-now-lead-8-points-conservatives-second-place-latest-voting-intention-polling)). Among issues "very important" to the next vote, the NHS and inflation (61% each) outrank immigration (52%) and the economy (45%) ([Ipsos Pulse Oct tables](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/news/documents/2026-10/Ipsos%20Political%20Pulse%20%28r1%29%20-%20October%202026%20-%20Tables.pdf)). ## 7 May: Reform wins 1,454 councillors, Plaid takes Wales, the SNP falls short

The English local elections were a Reform advance broader but shallower than 2025. Across 136 councils and 5,066 seats Reform won **1,454 councillors (+1,452) and 14 councils**, Labour 1,068 (−1,498) losing 38 councils, Conservatives 801 (−563), Lib Dems 844 (+155) and Greens 587 (+441, five councils), with 23 councils moving to no overall control; the BBC PNS was Reform 26 (−4 on 2025), Green 18 (+7), Labour 17, Conservative 17, Lib Dem 16, and Sky's national equivalent vote Reform 27, Con 20, Lab 15, Grn 14, LD 14 ([Wikipedia, 2026 UK local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_Kingdom_local_elections)). These figures supersede the "1,050 seats, 12 councils" and "NEV 28/21/18/14/13" numbers an earlier researcher took from Britpolls search summaries: Britpolls' live pages carry hallmarks of pre-written template text (polling day given as 1 May, "approximate" tables, a Holyrood hub still showing Labour "polling avg. 29%"), and its numbers match no primary source ([Britpolls results page](https://britpolls.co.uk/news/uk-local-elections-2026-results/)). John Curtice said the results "confirmed the fragmentation of our politics"; Reform won 47% of the vote where the 2016 Leave share exceeded 55% but 19% where it was below 45% ([Wikipedia, 2026 UK local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_Kingdom_local_elections)). London was Labour's worst since 1968: its councillors fell from 1,046 to 696 and its councils from 21 to 9, the Greens rose from 47 to 297 councillors and took Hackney, Lewisham and Waltham Forest plus the Hackney and Lewisham mayoralties, Reform took Havering, the Conservatives regained Westminster 32–22, and the London-wide vote was Labour 25.6, Green 22.3, Conservative 20.0, Reform 13.0, Lib Dem 12.4 ([Wikipedia, 2026 London local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_London_local_elections)). The main English polling miss was the Greens: Britain Elects and More in Common seat models had projected roughly 1,090 Green and 460–620 Labour councillors against the actual 587 and 1,068, consistent with late tactical consolidation behind Labour incumbents. YouGov's post-election poll found 2024 Labour voters who turned out split 46% Labour, 22% Green, 16% Lib Dem and 6% Reform — "almost 4 times as many to Greens as Reform" — leading Patrick English to conclude from ward data that "the Greens are a bigger threat to the Labour vote than Reform are" ([YouGov, local elections 2026](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/54811-labours-voter-coalition-broke-more-to-left-than-right-at-2026-local-elections)).

Wales was the clearest polling success of the night and the clearest Labour catastrophe. Polls under the new system moved from Plaid leads of 14 points in January to a near-tie in April (YouGov 29–29; Survation had Reform ahead 30–28), then the final polls bracketed the result: Find Out Now Plaid 35/Reform 28/Labour 9, YouGov 33/29/12, Survation 30/28/15 against actual **Plaid 35.4, Reform 29.3, Labour 11.1, Conservative 10.7, Green 6.7, Lib Dem 4.5** on a record 51.6% turnout ([Wikipedia, Opinion polling for the 2026 Senedd election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opinion_polling_for_the_2026_Senedd_election); [Wikipedia, 2026 Senedd election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Senedd_election)). YouGov's final MRP "exactly predicted the number of seats that Plaid, Reform, the Greens, Lib Dem and other parties would win" (43/34/2/1) ([YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/54735-yougovs-was-the-most-accurate-seat-projection-of-the-2026-welsh-senedd-election)). The ERS calculated a disproportionality (Loosemore–Hanby) score of 15.5, against 47.4 for the 2024 UK general election in Wales ([ERS, How Wales voted](https://electoral-reform.org.uk/how-wales-voted-in-the-2026-senedd-election/)). The Senedd Record shows ap Iorwerth nominated First Minister on 12 May by 44 votes to Reform's Dan Thomas 34 and Darren Millar 7, with nine abstentions (Labour and the sole Lib Dem) — the official 44, not the 45 in a Wikipedia summary ([Senedd Record, 12 May 2026](https://www.theyworkforyou.com/wales/?id=2026-05-12.3.762202.h)). By autumn Labour had recovered to 13–18% on Senedd VI (More in Common 18%, +7 since May) and 22% for Westminster in Wales, because only 63% of Plaid's Senedd voters would vote Plaid at a general election; Burnham was "the most popular leader in Wales" and Reform "would be the largest party in Wales at a general election" ([More in Common, Governing a fragmented Wales](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/PC-Coalition-Deck-2.pdf)). Dan Thomas resigned as Reform's Welsh leader in September and Helen Jenner replaced him; Ken Skates became Welsh Labour leader ([Wikipedia, Next Senedd election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Next_Senedd_election)).

Scotland was "the 'meh' election" in More in Common's phrase. The full Wikipedia series (34 constituency polls in 2026) has the SNP leading every poll by 12–24 points with Labour and Reform fighting for second in the high teens; the result was **SNP 38.2% constituency / 27.2% list, 58 seats; Labour 19.2/16.0, 17; Reform 15.8/16.7, 17 (no constituency seats); Greens 2.3/14.0, 15 (best ever, first constituency wins); Conservatives 11.8/11.8, 12 (worst ever); Lib Dems 11.4/9.4, 10; turnout 53.4% (−10.1)** ([Wikipedia, 2026 Scottish Parliament election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Scottish_Parliament_election)). The industry's miss was on seats: six of eight MRPs and aggregators projected an SNP majority or near-majority (59–67) against the actual 58, because the Greens' two constituency gains and the Lib Dems' four gains from the SNP were not visible in the models. Nine constituencies changed hands; the SNP gained Shetland from the Lib Dems and Eastwood from the Conservatives ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Scottish_Parliament_election)). Six post-election polls (June–October) show no Burnham bounce for Scottish Labour at Holyrood (15–21% constituency) and Reform steady at 13–19%; Anas Sarwar resigned and Michael Marra became Scottish Labour leader on 19 September; Reform MSP Amanda Bland left the party in October ([Wikipedia, next Scottish Parliament election polling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opinion_polling_for_the_next_Scottish_Parliament_election); [Wikipedia, Reform UK Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reform_UK_Scotland)). On independence, 30 polls in 2026 put Yes between 40% (YouGov) and 52% (Norstat, Stonehaven) with don't-knows included; Swinney's 21 May claim that "polls show support for independence is now in the majority" holds only for don't-know-excluded Norstat, Find Out Now and Stonehaven readings, never for YouGov or Ipsos, and the 8–10 point house gap between YouGov and Norstat dwarfs any 2026 movement ([Wikipedia, Opinion polling on Scottish independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opinion_polling_on_Scottish_independence); [Official Report, 21 May 2026](https://www.theyworkforyou.com/sp/?id=2026-05-21.4.0)).

All six 2026 Westminster by-elections are in the archive at candidate level from the UK Parliament Members API. Gorton and Denton (26 Feb, Green gain, majority 4,402, turnout 47.5%), Makerfield (18 Jun, Labour hold, majority 9,231, turnout 58.7%), Aberdeen South (18 Jun, Conservative gain from SNP, Douglas Lumsden 49.5%, +25.1, turnout 38.0%), Arbroath and Broughty Ferry (18 Jun, SNP hold, Lara Bird 41.2%, turnout 31.3%), Clacton (13 Aug, Reform hold, majority 12,784, turnout 44.0%) and Holborn and St Pancras (8 Oct, Labour hold, majority 3,364, turnout 38.6%) ([Members API results](https://members-api.parliament.uk/api/Location/Constituency/4066/ElectionResult/554); files `data/parliament_members_api_byelections_2026_*.csv`). ## Reform's coalition is old, non-graduate and splitting; Labour's lost left came back

The archive's demographic tables (nine YouGov VI PDFs from January to November, 44 More in Common and 31 Opinium workbooks, five Electoral Calculus MRP tables) show the age gradient flipping shape during 2026. In YouGov's 4–5 January tables Reform led every group over 50 (34% among 50–64s, 33% among 65+) and the Greens led the under-30s (38% of 18–24s); by 11–12 October the Conservatives led the 65+ band (34%, Reform 24), Labour led every band under 50 (37% of 18–24s, Greens 34), and Reform's best band was 50–64 at 25% ([YouGov tables, 4–5 Jan](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/VotingIntention_MRP_Results_260105_w.pdf); [YouGov tables, 11–12 Oct](https://ygo-assets-websites-editorial-emea.yougov.net/documents/VotingIntention_Results_261012_w.pdf)). More in Common's 9–12 October table has the Conservatives at 42% among over-75s and Labour at 42% among 25–34s ([MiC tables, 9–12 Oct](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Voting-Intention-Trackers-9-12-Oct.xlsx)). The gender gap is a Reform phenomenon on every table (men six to eleven points above women: YouGov January 30/22, October 25/17) while the Conservatives now do better among women (YouGov October 25 vs 21), and the Green coalition is the mirror image of Reform's — 18–24s, women (44% of young women vs 30% of young men in January), private renters (25%), graduates and Muslim voters ([YouGov, start of 2026](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/53923-how-would-britain-vote-at-the-start-of-2026)). Education is the sharpest Reform gradient of all: 40% among those qualified below A-level against 9% of graduates in the British Social Attitudes survey, 42% GCSE-or-lower against 13% of graduates in YouGov's merged January sample; YouGov notes that older professionals (27%) out-vote young routine workers (19%) for Reform, so age trumps class ([NatCen, Who supports Reform](https://natcen.ac.uk/news/support-reform-much-more-protest-vote-finds-new-british-social-attitudes-research); [YouGov, start of 2026](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/53923-how-would-britain-vote-at-the-start-of-2026)). Social renters were Reform's strongest tenure group (37% in January) and outright owners its second (32%) ([YouGov, start of 2026](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/53923-how-would-britain-vote-at-the-start-of-2026)).

Retention and flows explain the year's reversal more than conversion. YouGov's tables show 2024 Labour retention rising from 50% (January) to 60% (October) while Reform's retention of its own 2024 voters fell from 93% to 68% as 17% went to the Conservatives and 10% to Restore; More in Common's October table, which excludes don't-knows, has Labour retaining 73%, the Conservatives 74% and Reform 74% ([YouGov tables, 11–12 Oct](https://ygo-assets-websites-editorial-emea.yougov.net/documents/VotingIntention_Results_261012_w.pdf); [MiC tables, 9–12 Oct](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Voting-Intention-Trackers-9-12-Oct.xlsx)). YouGov's mid-2026 Big Voter Study is the key document on firmness: immigration is "the top factor in Reform's rise", with 61% of Labour-to-Reform switchers citing immigration still being too high and 92% of switchers trusting Reform over the Conservatives on it; cost of living matters far more to ex-Labour (51%) than ex-Tory (30%) Reform voters; and firmness is asymmetric — **73% of Conservative-to-Reform switchers are willing to "return home" but only 11% of Labour-to-Reform switchers would consider Labour again, and 52% of them score Labour 0/10** ([YouGov Big Voter Study, Reform](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55153-big-yougov-voter-study-2026-how-solid-is-reform-uks-rise); [YouGov Big Voter Study, Labour defectors](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55150-big-yougov-voter-study-2026-which-voters-abandoned-labour-over-keir-starmers-premiership-and-why)). Labour-to-Green switchers, by contrast, cited Gaza (23%), trans rights (17%) and immigration rhetoric (11%), and only 26% of Green voters had "no reservations" about the party ([YouGov Big Voter Study, Greens](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55154-big-yougov-voter-study-2026-what-do-green-voters-think-of-the-party)). The British Social Attitudes chapter finds Reform's rise "substantially driven by ideology" rather than service discontent: 75% of Reform supporters say migrants undermine British culture and 78% say unemployment benefits are too high, yet only 11% want less disability-benefit spending ([NatCen](https://natcen.ac.uk/news/support-reform-much-more-protest-vote-finds-new-british-social-attitudes-research)).

More in Common's British Seven segmentation (Progressive Activists 12%, Incrementalist Left 21%, Established Liberals 9%, Sceptical Scrollers 10%, Rooted Patriots 20%, Dissenting Disruptors 20%, Traditional Conservatives 8%) frames the mechanics of Reform's decline. Dissenting Disruptors — "the core of the populist right", of whom 94% say politicians lack respect for them, 51% think Britain could face civil war within ten years and 45% did not vote in 2024 — made up half of Reform's 2024 vote and 43% of its 2026 support, but Reform's share among them fell 14–15 points over the year to 44% as 11% moved to Restore and others to abstention, and Farage's net rating with them fell from above +30 to +7 ([MiC, Dissenting Disruptors](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/dissenting-disruptors-more-in-common.pdf); [MiC Reform deck](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Reform-Deck-Full.pdf)). At the other end the Conservatives now lead Reform 45–37 among Traditional Conservatives and trail by only four points among Rooted Patriots (18 a year earlier), where Farage is at −15; "the right is increasingly divided, while the Left is uniting" ([MiC Conservative deck](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Conservative-Deck-2026-2-1.pdf)). Labour's June coalition report found 37% of its 2024 voters would not vote Labour again, with Labour–Reform switchers disproportionately Rooted Patriots (32%) and Progressive Activists swinging 14 points away, 40% of them believing Labour had been more right-wing than expected ([MiC, Labour's coalition](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/labour-s-coalition.pdf)). Reform's 2026 coalition is therefore three blocs with different exits — soft ex-Tories returning to Badenoch, hardened immigration-first ex-Labour voters who are not returning, and a radical core being poached by Restore and abstention — and the autumn decline came from the first and third. Restore's own supporters are defined by trust on immigration (48%) and near-universal warmth for Lowe (88% very favourable); Electoral Calculus found 40% of Reform voters "likely" to vote Restore, and More in Common's September MRP identified 76 seats where Reform finishes second by less than Restore's share ([YouGov Big Voter Study, Restore](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55157-big-yougov-voter-study-2026-what-do-restore-britain-supporters-see-in-the-party); [EC Sep 2026](https://www.electoralcalculus.co.uk/blogs/ec_vipoll_20260924.html); [MiC Sep MRP](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/research/more-in-commons-september-2026-mrp/)).

## Focus groups: "I've never earned as much, but I've never felt as poor"

More in Common ran groups "every week" and published at least 20 qualitative outputs in 2026, all primary-sourced and saved as PDFs in `data/focus_groups/`; it does not publish transcripts, but its by-election reports and conference decks carry dozens of attributed verbatims each ([More in Common research listing](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-json/wp/v2/research?after=2026-01-01T00:00:00&per_page=100)). The year's qualitative arc has three acts. In the Starmer phase voters described terminal disillusion and "roll the dice politics — because few think that things can get much worse": in the three Gorton and Denton results-night groups Burnham was "the most popular politician across the groups, with both Reform voters and Green voters", Starmer "almost uniformly … personally unlikeable", and a Reform voter explained "none of them are winners, let's be honest … Labour have just made an absolute arse of it. The Tories have made an arse of it. I thought I would try something new" ([MiC, Gorton and Denton dispatch](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/gorton-and-denton-by-election-dispatch.pdf)). The Respect Crisis groups (February) found only 19% think the government respects people like them and 72% saying Mandelson showed "one rule for those with wealth and status"; the pre-election groups framed England as "the insurgent election", Scotland "the 'meh' election" and Wales "the change election", with a Wrexham hospitality worker saying "absolutely anything to give Labour a bloody nose right now I think will be a good thing" and a Cardiff participant describing Welsh Labour as "this elite kind of political class that nobody's really in touch with" ([MiC, A Respect Crisis](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/respect-final.pdf); [MiC, No Overall Control](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/no-overall-control_-british-politics-in-an-era-of-fragmentation-1.pdf)). The Winter Fuel Allowance cut remained Starmer's original sin ("The first thing he did when he came to power was take away the pensioners' winter allowance") and Labour was accused of having "just stolen the name" ([MiC, Makerfield](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Makerfield-Report-typeset.pdf)).

The 11 Makerfield groups are the richest single document. Burnham "defied electoral gravity through his own personal brand" — "I'm not a Labour fan at all, but I am a fan of Andy Burnham"; "It was purely, are you voting for Andy? Not even Burnham"; "I've seen him at Asda in Golborne shopping … It's not for effect" — with the Greater Manchester bus-fare cap the single most-cited proof that the state can function ("saved me about 60 quid a month"), while Reform's Robert Kenyon was "out of his depth completely" and his misogynistic posts split groups by generation ([MiC, Makerfield](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Makerfield-Report-typeset.pdf)). The report's central finding is that "some voters were voting Reform in spite of Nigel Farage, not because of him": "The prospect of Nigel Farage as prime minister is frankly quite terrifying … even more sycophantic as far as Trump's concerned"; "he lied to us about Brexit"; and Restore was already registering as "a bit more sensible for me" even among those who voted Reform "because I didn't want to split the vote". Badenoch drew warmth without conversion — "I could never vote Conservative, but I like her … she wipes the floor with Keir Starmer"; "Margaret Thatcher with humour" — a pattern Luke Tryl later summarised as "real praise for Badenoch but so quickly tempered by an unwillingness to forgive her party" and the October Conservative deck captured as "head of an empty party" ([MiC, Makerfield](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Makerfield-Report-typeset.pdf); [MiC Conservative deck](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Conservative-Deck-2026-2-1.pdf)). The "squeezed middle" frame runs from the Stoke and Perry Barr energy groups in March ("It's either heat or food. You can't do both") through Makerfield ("I've never earned as much money in my work, but I've never felt as poor"; "People on our street who have the council houses and all the benefits, they live the same life [as us]. I'm paying for it") to the pre-Budget groups ("Just the idiots in the middle we're the only ones paying tax") ([MiC, Energy bills tracker](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/eb-energy-bills-sentiment-tracker-march_april-2026.pdf); [MiC, Pre-Budget Briefing](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/MIC-Budget-Briefing-Deck-2026-1.pdf)). Misinformation about Burnham "has come up unprompted consistently in focus groups we've run in Makerfield, seemingly largely from Facebook", a finding quoted in the Lords and later followed by Burnham's pledge of a national anti-disinformation centre ([Lords Hansard, 25 Jun 2026](https://www.theyworkforyou.com/lords/?id=2026-06-25b.791.0)).

The Burnham-era groups show a conditional honeymoon. UCL Policy Lab and More in Common found him described as "Good", "ambitious", "Northern" but "unknown", with Britons giving him "up to a year to make tangible changes" and Labour switchers "six months to bring down energy bills"; a Manchester participant found it "sort of miraculous" that "Reform voters … like him and … people that vote Green also quite like him" ([MiC/UCL, Britons and Burnham](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/July-2026-Burnham-Report.pdf)). In Clacton Farage "splits" the town — "I love him to bits" against "in two years, what's he done for Clacton?" and "You can't really say that Farage is not a member of the establishment, considering how often he's at Ascot" — and a follow-up poll found 49% wanting a second by-election if he was found to have broken rules ([MiC, Clacton in their own words](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/research/the-clacton-by-election-in-their-own-words/); [MiC, Public reactions to Clacton](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/research/public-reactions-to-the-clacton-by-election/)). Lord Ashcroft's September rounds (Leeds, Halesowen, Solihull, Croydon, Crawley, Reading) carried the same verdict in their headline quotes — "Show us what your plan is, at least. Then we can see if you achieve any of it" and "You're changing the captain of the Titanic after it's hit the iceberg" — though none of his eight 2026 write-ups could be opened and they remain search-snippet pointers ([Lord Ashcroft, Sep 2026](https://lordashcroftpolls.com/2026/09/show-us-what-your-plan-is-at-least-then-we-can-see-if-you-achieve-any-of-it-my-latest-focus-groups/)). The Green groups in Holborn, Sheffield Heeley and Brighton showed the party's drift from climate to Palestine and trans issues costing it long-standing supporters ("I've voted Green for years and years … He says what he thinks will make him popular"; "Stop and get on with the climate"; "I wouldn't want to [vote] for somebody who's antisemitic") ([MiC, Where next for the Greens](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/green-deck-2026.pdf)). The post-Budget groups in Rawmarsh and Conisbrough and Portsmouth confirmed welfare fairness as the fault line: "it's meant to be there as a safety net, and it's not a safety net. And for some, it is a bloody lilo"; the Budget "sounded to me like a stop gap … a lot of tinkering"; 46% thought Burnham "does what is popular rather than what is right", only 24% believed the government had a clear plan to cut the benefits bill, but there was "no backlash this time" — 27% said it made them less likely to trust Labour on the economy against 43% a year earlier, and Burnham's net moved from +8 to +13 ([MiC/UCL, Post-Budget Debrief](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Post-Budget-Webinar.pdf)). Broadcast and press voter panels (FT's 13 Sheffield swing voters at Burnham's 100 days, Sky, ITV, Channel 4, Politico and Telegraph films from Makerfield and Gorton) exist only as video metadata in the archive ([FT Sheffield group](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=js9LiuZLQJs)).

## Strategic reading: 2026 tested the progressive-defector thesis and largely vindicated it

The strategic argument of early 2026 was whether Labour should chase its Reform defectors or its progressive ones. Persuasion UK and 38 Degrees' "Revolt on the Left" (March, fielded Dec 2025–Jan 2026) found that for every two 2024 Labour voters lost to the right, three were lost to the left, that 55% of the remaining Labour vote was open to switching left against 21% right, that 138 battleground seats made progressive defectors Labour's most valuable group, and that a crude "lesser of two evils" squeeze message would not work, while closer EU ties, confident climate signalling and child-poverty progress worked "disproportionately well" ([Persuasion UK](https://persuasionuk.org/research/revolt-on-the-left); [LabourList](https://labourlist.org/2026/03/revolt-left-matthew-mcgregor-38-degrees/)). Its May post-election analysis found Labour losing 16 votes to progressive parties for every 10 to the right, with left defectors seeing the government as "Tory lite" but open to returning; the Fabians' "Learning Lessons" put it as one in twenty 2024 Labour voters gone to Reform against one in three to the Greens or Lib Dems ([Persuasion UK, May 2026](https://persuasionuk.org/research/may-2026-elections); [LabourList, Fabians](https://labourlist.org/2026/05/fabian-society-learning-lessons/)). Luke Akehurst's counter-case that "only Reform voters can save Labour" was contested by UCL Policy Lab data showing just 1% of Reform voters open to Labour against 13% of Greens and 15% of Lib Dems, and by YouGov's 11% figure for Labour-to-Reform switchers ([New Statesman, Akehurst](https://www.newstatesman.com/politics/uk-politics/2026/01/only-reform-voters-can-save-labour); [New Statesman, UCL](https://www.newstatesman.com/politics/uk-politics/may-2026/2026/05/just-1-per-cent-of-reform-voters-would-consider-supporting-labour)). IPPR and Persuasion UK's joint testing found voters would punish fiscal rules without visible service improvement and that aggressive attack messages did not damage Labour's economic credibility ([IPPR](https://www.ippr.org/media-office/voters-want-the-government-to-put-fixing-public-services-ahead-of-balancing-books-new-polling-finds)).

By November the Burnham bounce had, in effect, run that experiment. Labour's recovery to 26–32% came primarily from returning Green, Lib Dem and undecided voters — the Greens are down ten points since March, Labour's 2024 retention is up ten points on YouGov, and Ipsos's Labour-to-Green leakage fell from 12% to 5% in a month — while Reform's seven-point fall since May went mostly to the Conservatives and Restore, not Labour; Electoral Calculus's August transition analysis found that of 100 voters from 2024 "only one voter has crossed over between left and right" ([Ipsos, 22 Oct 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/en-uk/labour-now-lead-8-points-conservatives-second-place-latest-voting-intention-polling); [EC voter transition](https://www.electoralcalculus.co.uk/blogs/pseph_transition2026aug.html)). Tactical voting has become the second structural asset. YouGov's September tactical landscape found 52–55% of Lib Dem and Green voters would back Labour to stop the Conservatives and 63–68% to stop Reform, with 2024 Labour voters willing to vote Conservative against Reform rising from 20% to 35% in a year; Electoral Calculus's October study (n=10,684) found 50% of Labour supporters would vote Conservative to defeat Reform (38% in 2025), "almost all Green supporters would vote Labour to defeat Reform", and the net seat effect against Reform ran from 63 seats in January and 85 in April to about 20 in October as Reform's raw share fell ([YouGov tactical Sep 2026](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/55613-what-is-the-tactical-voting-landscape-in-september-2026); [EC tactical voting and PR 2026](https://www.electoralcalculus.co.uk/blogs/pseph_tactical_2026.html)). Hope not Hate's "Blocking the Path" found 43% would be unhappy with a Reform government and 69% of them willing to vote tactically, with 95 "fragile" Reform seats ([Left Foot Forward](https://leftfootforward.org/2026/08/voters-unhappy-at-idea-of-reform-government-could-block-nigel-farage-from-winning-power-hope-not-hate-survey-finds/)). The same Electoral Calculus data carry a warning for Burnham's electoral-reform pledge: under party-list PR Reform would win 180 seats and a Reform–Conservative–Restore bloc 332, against 339 for Labour, Lib Dems and Greens under FPTP with tactical voting ([EC tactical voting and PR 2026](https://www.electoralcalculus.co.uk/blogs/pseph_tactical_2026.html)).

The unresolved test is the squeezed middle. More in Common's pre-Budget briefing found Labour leading among both the comfortable and the struggling but trailing among those "who can cover their essentials but for whom luxuries seem more and more out of reach", with 57% of £20–40k earners saying they pay too much tax; the most popular taxes were on vape shops (+60), energy companies (+58), banks (+56) and a 1% wealth tax over £10m (+55) ([MiC, Pre-Budget Briefing](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/research/more-in-commons-pre-budget-briefing/)). Welfare is where that group's grievance concentrates: 65% say social security spending is out of control, but roughly six in ten prefer tackling underlying causes to cutting eligibility, and Tryl's judgement that a "control plan" on welfare "will be a key credibility test for all parties" is borne out by the post-Budget finding that only 24% see such a plan ([MiC, Causes over Cuts](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/research/causes-over-cuts/); [MiC/UCL, Post-Budget Debrief](https://www.moreincommon.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Post-Budget-Webinar.pdf)). Two further constraints come out of the segment data: the Burnham government's approval is still read through "the Labour government since 2024" unless the question names him, and the Green coalition that has partly returned to Labour did so without any Labour shift on immigration, which suggests the Persuasion/Fabian warning — that out-Reforming Reform "will not win back Reform supporters, but it will alienate progressive voters" — remains the operative risk.

## Conclusion

The 2026 record overturns the assumption, universal in January, that Reform's lead was structural. It was broad but shallow: concentrated among over-50s, non-graduates and a 20% "Dissenting Disruptor" segment whose loyalty ran to Farage's insurgency rather than to Farage, and it fractured the moment a credible alternative appeared on the right (Restore, a recovering Badenoch) and a likeable one on the centre-left (Burnham). The more durable lesson for progressive campaigners is about the left's own coalition: Labour's 2024 voters who went Green or Lib Dem were recoverable without a single concession on immigration, those who went to Reform were not, and the electorate's willingness to vote tactically against Reform grew all year even as Reform's share fell — a combination that, under first-past-the-post, converts a 26–28% Labour vote into a plausible majority but would hand the right a majority under the party-list PR Burnham has promised to put in a manifesto.

What the data cannot yet show is whether the honeymoon survives contact with delivery. Every qualitative source gives Burnham six to twelve months to make energy bills, waiting lists and welfare fairness tangibly different, the government's institutional approval still trails his personal rating by 30–40 net points, and the Budget confirmed his strengths ("gets it") while deepening the "people pleaser" doubt. The highest-value additions to this archive before the next round of analysis are the LucidTalk autumn tracker, the BES Wave 31 microdata for a proper switcher model, the YouGov tracker workbooks for weekly demographic series, and the Ashcroft and Persuasion UK documents that would let the qualitative and message-testing evidence be read from more than one vendor.
