Foreword
For the last month, politics and public life in Northern Ireland have been dominated by Drumcree. Across radio,
television, newspaper columns and social media feeds, there has been room for little else. There is good reason for this, as
the dispute raises important questions that go well beyond one road or one parade in Portadown. It speaks to how far
Northern Ireland has come, or not come, since 1998; why disputes seem settled for some people and not for others; and
what identity, culture and respect mean for different communities in Northern Ireland in the 2020s.

Debates like this need to hear from a wide range of voices: the people willing to go on air and talk about their views,
those willing to turn up to a rally in support of one side or the other, and those happy to share their views online about
Drumcree and what they think it means more broadly. But these more certain, and sometimes louder, voices are only part
of the picture on what Northern Ireland thinks on Drumcree.

This report is More in Common’s contribution to understanding how people in Northern Ireland, drawn from a
representative sample of the public, are thinking about Drumcree and navigating the debates around it. The poll
was ﬁelded in the days after the Secretary of State’s decision to block the parade. It examines attitudes to Drumcree
itself and explores views on decisions taken by politicians, the Parades Commission and other institutions across
Northern Ireland. It also tries to go upstream and ask a series of broader questions about what the resurfacing of the
Drumcree dispute means for Northern Ireland in the years ahead.

Foreword
Some of what we found will surprise few. Most Catholics side with the Garvaghy Road residents, while most Protestants
think the Secretary of State was wrong to block the parade. Each community believes the Parades Commission favours
the other side, and Catholics believe the same of the PSNI. The divides on Drumcree, like the divides on many of the
issues that matter most in Northern Ireland, are deep, and this research does not ﬁnd otherwise.

But nor do we ﬁnd a public split neatly down the middle. Almost three in ﬁve Protestants say they understand why the
Garvaghy Road residents feel as they do, and over half of Catholics say the same about the Portadown Orangemen. Asked
what matters most in how the dispute ends, people of every party and both communities give the same answer: that it
ends without violence. And of the seven ways of ending the dispute we tested, one is acceptable to voters of every party
– the Lodge’s right to parade is conﬁrmed, and it chooses not to use it.

Our hope is that this research does justice to the different shades of opinion on Drumcree: that it takes seriously the
divisions between communities here, and takes just as seriously the common ground between them.

More in Common will return to these questions as we build out our work in Northern Ireland, with deeper studies on
culture and commemoration and on migration planned in the months ahead, and more detailed political and electoral
analysis in the run-up to the Assembly election.

Executive Summary
Where the public stand on Drumcree
Most think the Parades Commission got it wrong and the Secretary of State got it right. Half (53%) say the Commission’s
determination was wrong and the same proportion (53%) back the Secretary of State’s decision to block the parade on the
Garvaghy Road. Nationalist and Alliance voters overwhelmingly support the Secretary of State’s decision; unionist voters mostly
think it was the wrong decision, but Protestants as a whole are more divided (54% wrong, 28% right).

More sympathise with the Garvaghy Road residents (38%) than the Portadown Orangemen (21%), but two in ﬁve pick
neither. Among Protestants, more don’t take a side (44%) than back the Orangemen (42%). TUV voters strongly side with the
Orangemen (79%), as do DUP voters (59%), but half of UUP voters don’t pick a side.

Most understand the other side’s perspective in the dispute. Almost three in ﬁve (57%) Protestants say they understand
why the Garvaghy Road residents feel as they do and over half (51%) of Catholics say the same of the Orangemen. Those
following the dispute most closely are more likely to say they understand where the other side are coming from.

Avoiding violence matters far more to the public than the parade going ahead or not. Four in ﬁve (82%) say it matters that
the dispute ends without violence, with two in three voters of every party saying it matters a great deal. The gap between the
most and least supportive voter groups is just 15 points on an issue where gaps elsewhere can run up to 60 or 70 points.

Of the seven ways to end the dispute that we tested, only one
is acceptable to voters of every party: the Portadown Lodge’s
right to parade is conﬁrmed, but it chooses not to use it.

Half the public (50%) say it would be an acceptable way to
end the dispute, against 16% who say unacceptable – and the
ﬁgures are the same for Catholics and Protestants (51% and
52% acceptable).

By voters of different parties this proposal is acceptable to a
majority of Sinn Féin (56%), Alliance (75%), UUP (62%) and DUP
voters (55%), and a plurality of SDLP (44%) and TUV voters
(45%). For SDLP voters, it falls short of a majority mainly
because more answered “don’t know” or “neither acceptable
nor unacceptable”.

Support is lower among the under-35s (42%) and those not
following the dispute (36%), mostly because more of them are
unsure, not because more oppose it.

The other six options tested are unacceptable to one side or the

Three in ﬁve people in Northern Ireland (60%) say the

Whoever is marching, most say the organisers should
ﬁnd another route
In recent weeks, there has been some debate over whether
unionists would accept a republican parade marching through a
predominantly unionist area. More in Common explored this idea
by presenting respondents with two mirror-image scenarios.


Overall, in both scenarios, around a third say that the parade
should go ahead, around half say the police should not
remove protesting residents, and two in three say that
organisers should offer another route.


Catholics and Protestants start from a different position on
parading. Catholics are against contentious parades in both

Protestants are more likely to back an orange parade in a

Fewer than one in three want the Portadown Orangemen
to keep applying to parade on Garvaghy Road
With the courts upholding their right to parade and the
Secretary of State blocking it under section 11 of the
Public Processions (NI) Act 1998, the Portadown Lodge
faces a choice on whether or not to continue trying to
parade down the Garvaghy Road. Three in ﬁve (60%)
say they should accept the decision and not apply
again, while fewer than a third (29%) say they should
keep pressing.

Nationalist and Alliance voters overwhelmingly want the
Orangemen to accept the decision and not press for
future parades. Most TUV and DUP voters want the
Orangemen to keep pressing for a Garvaghy Road parade.
UUP voters are more evenly divided - 47% want them to
keep pressing, while 41% would prefer that they did not.

Latent Class Analysis Methodology note
The input was 14 questions from the poll: who respondents sympathise with, whether the Secretary of State and the Parades
Commission were right or wrong, whether the Lodge should keep pressing or accept the decision, whether one side is right or
both have a case, whether the dispute should be settled soon or on their community’s terms, whether Commission rulings
should be followed or resisted, and whether each of seven possible endings to the dispute would be acceptable or
unacceptable. Each answer was collapsed to three levels – one side, the other side, or neither / don’t know – so that the
groups reﬂect which way people lean, not how strongly.

Models with one to six groups were ﬁtted to all 1,019 respondents, weighted as in the published ﬁgures. Fit statistics kept
improving as groups were added, as they usually do with this many questions, so the choice of four rests on interpretability: a
ﬁfth group split an existing one without adding a distinct pattern of views. The four-group solution separates people cleanly –
on average, respondents are placed in their group with 95 to 97 per cent certainty, and around nine in ten (85% to 93%,
depending on the group) are placed with better than 90 per cent certainty. Overall classiﬁcation certainty (entropy) is 0.94,
where 1 would mean every respondent placed with no doubt.

The groups were then named by More in Common’s researchers.
