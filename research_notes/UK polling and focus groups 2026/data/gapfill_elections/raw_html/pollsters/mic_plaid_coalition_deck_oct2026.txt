Plaid Cymru won by building two voter bases
Plaid Cymru was propelled by a widespread desire for change in
Wales. It held on to its traditional base of older, Welsh-speaking
voters in the rural North and West - and added a wave of younger,
urban defectors from Labour, comparable to Green voters in England.

As a result, Plaid's voter base has changed. Its support is less
closely tied to Welsh-speaking areas, its voters are more left-wing
than in 2021, and its supporters are almost as likely to have voted
Labour in 2024 (34 per cent) as Plaid Cymru (40 per cent). The party
now, in effect, has two voter groups to balance.

Wales is still waiting to see
A plurality of people in Wales say Plaid Cymru's time in
government has been about what they expected (41 per
cent), while 25 per cent say it has been worse and 17 per
cent better. Seven in ten (70 per cent) say the new
government feels like more of the same compared to the
previous Welsh Labour government.
  Plaid Cymru's start to government




Rhun ap Iorwerth is not yet well known, but the public want to give him a hearing

Five months in, many voters are still making up their minds about the
First Minister. But he has clear strengths as a leader.

Three in ten (30 per cent) don't know whether he is doing a good or bad
job. His net approval in Wales (-3) is six points behind Andy Burnham
(+3).

But where voters do know him, they often warm to him. He is the only
politician tested who more people think respects the people of Wales
than not (48 per cent to 28 per cent) - including majorities of Labour
(68 per cent) and Conservative (52 per cent) voters, and a plurality of
Reform UK voters (39 per cent)..

Popular policies, but struggling to cut through
Okaud Cymru’s opening policy agenda has been popular, but
many of their most positive policies have gone under the
radar.

No policy or moment tested has been heard of by more than 54
per cent of people in Wales.

The best-known moves are constitutional - the independence
pact (54 per cent) and the request for referendum powers (48 per
cent) - and they split voters almost evenly.

The exception is cost of living support: free school meals (51 per
cent aware, 58 per cent positive) and £1 bus fares (50 per cent,
59 per cent) are both well known and well liked.

But the best-rated pledge - promising up to 10 new NHS surgical
hubs (60 per cent positive) - has been heard of by just 36 per
cent. The supplementary budget defeat is the only moment seen
more negatively than positively.

Two key tests for this government
Across every voter group in Wales, the public say they will
judge the Plaid Cymru Government on two key tests: how
much they lower the cost of living (59 per cent) and and how
they reduce NHS waiting lists (55 per cent). These are the top
two priorities for Plaid Cymru, Labour, Reform UK and
Conservative voters alike. They are well ahead of jobs and
opportunities for young people (25 per cent), affordable housing
(18 per cent) and child poverty (17 per cent).



By contrast, few say they will judge the government on
constitutional questions. Fewer than one in ten say they will
judge it on whether it moves Wales closer to independence (9 per
cent), gets more powers devolved to Wales (9 per cent) or
protects the Welsh language (8 per cent). Even among Plaid
Cymru's own voters, only 15 per cent say independence will be
one of their main tests.

Plaid Cymru hold their lead in the Senedd
Plaid Cymru holds a ﬁve-point lead over Reform UK for
the Senedd, ﬁve months on from winning May's Senedd
election. Labour has recovered seven points since the
election, while both Plaid Cymru (-3) and Reform UK (-2)
have slipped back.
For Plaid Cymru, this suggests that their honeymoon period
has not dissipated: ﬁve months on, their vote share has only
dropped by three points.
And while Labour’s climb seems to suggest a ‘Burnham
Bounce’, they have not recovered: an election held tomorrow
would still be their second worst Welsh election in history.

But the ‘Burnham Bounce’ could split Plaid’s vote in Westminster
Labour polls four points higher in Wales for Westminster (22 per cent) than
for the Senedd (18 per cent), largely because Plaid Cymru voters split their
vote.

Only 63 per cent of those backing Plaid for the Senedd would vote Plaid at a
general election. Almost one in ﬁve (18 per cent) would vote Labour, and 7 per
cent Green. Other parties keep their voters: more than nine in ten Labour (95 per
cent) and Conservative (94 per cent) Senedd voters back the same party at
Westminster, as do 86 per cent of Reform voters.

This is the Burnham bounce at work. Andy Burnham is popular with Plaid voters
(+37), and at Westminster he is drawing back some of the progressives Plaid won
from Labour.
