Can Badenoch turn the Conservatives around?




Kemi Badenoch has broken through - but has not lifted her
party’s fortunes
A year ago, the Conservatives’ main challenge was for
Kemi Badenoch to get a hearing; now it’s turning
Badenoch fans into Conservative voters.

Kemi Badenoch’s approval rating has soared in recent
months; she is now one of only two party leaders with a net
positive approval, and Reform voters in particular have
warmed to her.


But much as Andy Burnham is outperforming the Labour
Party, so too is Kemi Badenoch outperforming the
Conservatives. Attitudes toward the Conservative leader
have decoupled from support for her party: of all those
who believe Kemi Badenoch is doing a good job, only a
third (34 per cent) say they would vote Conservative in
an election tomorrow.
Can Badenoch turn the Conservatives around?




Some see Badenoch as a ‘breath of fresh air’
In focus groups, many voters on the right describe
Badenoch as a refreshing politician, someone unafraid to
‘say it as it is’.

In particular, her performances in the House of Commons
have helped her to break through as an effective Opposition
leader. Two in ﬁve Britons say they have seen Kemi
Badenoch doing PMQs in the past year, and those who have
are far more likely to describe Badenoch as ‘intelligent’,
‘professional’ and ‘principled’.

The Conservative brand is showing signs of recovery
On a handful of key metrics, perceptions of the Conservative
Party have improved since last year’s party conference.

Since last year the proportion saying that the Conservatives feel
‘genuinely different’ from when Rishi Sunak was leader has risen
from 25 per cent to 38 per cent now.

The number saying that the Conservatives position is ‘irrecoverable’
has fallen from 44 per cent to 40 per cent.

And since May of this year, the share who think the Conservatives
are the main party of the right has risen from 17 per cent to 25 per
cent.

The Battle for the Right is neck-and-neck
A year ago, Reform held over 60 per cent of the right-bloc vote.
Today it is level with the Conservatives (44 per cent to 45 per
cent), with Restore Britain on 11 per cent.

Perceptions are shifting too: since May, the share of Britons naming
Reform as the main party on the right has fallen from 43 per cent to
35%, while the Conservatives have risen from 17 per cent to 25 per
cent. The shift has been biggest among those who backed the
Conservatives in 2024.
Can Badenoch turn the Conservatives around?




Badenoch is performing well across the right and centre
The Conservative Party is expanding its support towards
both the centre and the right. It has reconnected with its
core segment, Traditional Conservatives. After trailing
Reform UK in this group for most of the past year, it now
leads by 45 per cent to 37 per cent. Among Established
Liberals, the Conservatives are once again tied with Labour,
by 34 per cent to 33 per cent. Among Rooted Patriots,
Reform UK's lead over the Conservatives has narrowed from
18 points to just four.


Badenoch is net popular with three of the seven segments-
Traditional Conservatives, Rooted Patriots, and Established
Liberals. She is also close behind with the Incrementalist Left
and Dissenting Disruptors.

The Conservatives have a high ceiling
With 22 per cent of the vote, the Conservatives are ﬁve
points behind Labour. An election tomorrow would likely be
even worse than their historic 2024 loss. But alongside
those currently supporting the party, an extra 22 per
cent of likely voters would consider backing them.


Taking current voters and considerers together, 44 per cent
of voters are reachable for the Conservatives in theory -
compared to 45 per cent for Labour and 38 per cent for
Reform UK.

The 2019 coalition is shattered presently by Reform, but
there are signs of recovery
The enormity of the shock delivered to the Conservatives in
2024 has not dissipated and the Conservative coalition in
2019 is far from being reconstructed.

Just 44 per cent who voted for the Conservatives under

Labour, but the biggest movement is still to the right.

A notable gender gap has started to emerge between

Deprivation is the deﬁning dividing line on the battle for
the right
The battle for the right is broadly breaking down along
lines of deprivation: the Conservatives do best in the least
deprived wards, and Reform in the more deprived ones.

In the least deprived tenth of wards, the Conservatives
won 36% of the councillors up for election in May, with
26% of the vote. In the most deprived tenth, that falls to
just 3% of councillors and 9% of the vote.


Reform, by contrast, do best in more deprived areas. They
won 15% of the vote in the least deprived wards, rising to
a peak of 27% in the eighth decile of deprivation, and won
between a third and 37% of councillors (33-37%) in the
seventh, eighth and ninth deciles.

Conservatives have become the party of the white
collar homeowner
Looking at local results in 2026 more closely, Conservative
voters on some metrics look similar to the Liberal Democrats,
and on others to Reform.

Like Reform, the Conservatives do well in whiter areas, do well

If replicated at a general election, this support proﬁle would

Conservatives could proﬁt from persuading Liberal
Democrats
At the moment, 24 per cent of Liberal Democrats are
open to considering voting Conservative.

A swing from the Liberal Democrats to Conservatives
could not only help win back Con-LD seats, but would win
back other seats where Liberal Democrats are likely
splitting the centre-right vote. A 1% swing takes the
Conservatives to 154 seats, larger than Reform, and a 3%
swing reduces the Liberal Democrats to 41 seats and
pushes the Conservatives above 200.

Conversely, the Conservatives are still vulnerable to losing
voters to the Liberal Democrats. Just a 2.8% swing from
Conservative to Liberal Democrat would leave the Liberal
Democrats larger than the Conservatives.

One way the Conservatives could establish a poll lead is
by squeezing the 'Tory curious' voters currently voting

If 50% of those voters would switch over, Conservatives
would lead Labour and Reform, 28% to 26% to 18%. This

a wide margin, with 302 seats, Labour on 238, Liberal
Democrats at 44, and Reform collapsing to just 3.

This coalition would be built overwhelmingly outside
London: the party's gains are concentrated in the South
East (63 seats), East of England (51), West Midlands (37)
and South West (37), and the East Midlands (33) with
smaller gains even reaching into Yorkshire and the

  But they cannot afford to sacriﬁce the centre
However, losing half of the Conservative voters who would
consider voting Labour (21%) or the Liberal Democrats (24%)
by adopting a strategy focusing on the right, could also put
off left leaning voters and also lead to a severe defeat.

Labour’s tally would rise to 340 seats, and the Conservatives
would fall to 177 seats. Reform would remain wiped out at just
3, and the Liberal Democrats would rise to 71. It would see
the Conservative coalition shrink hardest in London (down to
just 10 seats) and the North East (down to 1), while holding up
comparatively better in the East of England (38) and South
East (34).

This underscores the danger of adopting a purely right or left
leaning strategy. Losing either of the left or right ﬂanks would
both end in disaster. Conservatives must balance the need
to please the centre, and win back the right.
