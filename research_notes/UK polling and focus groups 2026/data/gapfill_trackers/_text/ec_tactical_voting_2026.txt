
Tactical Voting 2026
Tactical Voting and PR 2026
This page first posted 21 October 2026, reposted with corrections 22 October 2026
Electoral Calculus and 
Find Out Now
 have run a
major new poll of over 10,000 respondents
looking at both tactical voting and proportional representation electoral systems.
Which parties would benefit from these effects or changes?
Summary headlines:
Anti-Reform tactical voting is strong and could cost Reform around 20 seats.	
A Party List PR system would benefit the right, with a likely three-way coalition of
	Reform, Conservatives and Restore having a majority.
The alternative vote (AV) system would benefit right-of-centre parties more compared with
	FPTP and tactical voting.
FPTP with tactical voting would benefit the left the most, with a coalition between Labour and the Lib Dems able to secure a majority.
1. Introduction
The 2024 election was highly disproportionate, with Labour taking a sweeping majority on just 35pc of the vote while Reform UK and the Greens won a fifth of the vote combined but barely won any seats. This was down to how 
the first-past-the-post (FPTP) system works by rewarding large parties and those with geographically concentrated support, at a time when voters are spreading their votes across more parties than ever.
Andy Burnham has promised that Labour will fight the next general election on changing the voting system, and he has backed proportional representation before. The Liberal Democrats and the Greens strongly support a change, but the right, especially the Conservatives, is likely to resist. The two alternatives analysed by this poll are closed party list proportional 
representation (PR) and the alternative vote (AV) system.
Tactical voting is becoming much more significant amongst voters, with more choice of parties shaping how many people vote, but which parties would actually gain or lose if either alternative replaced the current system of FPTP?
To cast light on this, we have worked with Find Out Now to ask the 
public about tactical voting and alternative voting methods. 
We asked people if and how they might vote tactically, and how they would vote
under alternative voting systems such as Party List and Alternative Vote (AV).
Find Out Now interviewed 10,684 adults from 22 September to 12 October 2026.
The questions asked were:
Likelihood to vote, to focus on those voters likely to vote
Current voting intention (benchmark)
Hypothetical voting intention to discover true 'first preference' party
Various hypothetical A/B scenarios about how people would vote if there were only two (specified)
parties who are competitive in their seat.
Hypothetical voting intention under a proportional representation system, where voters rank
	parties
The aim is to find out, for example, how many Labour supporters might vote in a seat which is a contest between
the Conservatives and Reform. Would any Labour supporters vote Tory to keep Reform out? And what would
the results be under a different electoral system?

3. Tactical Voting
We asked our respondents if and how they might vote tactically to help their favoured parties, and
to hurt their disliked parties.
We performed this tactical voting analysis in a similar way to 
our 
2025 tactical voting poll
.
Voters were initially asked "Q3. Suppose all of the following political parties were equally likely to win your seat at a general election. Which party would you most like to win?". This question tries to discover
the voters true first preference choice party.
Given that, we then ask a number of A/B scenario questions, such as "Q4. CON-Reform": 
"Suppose your seat is a close contest between Conservative Party and Reform UK and no other party is likely to win. How would you vote in this case?"
The pairs used for the A/B scenarios vary across England, Scotland and Wales. They are listed in 
Appendix 2 for reference.
Some interesting insights from these A/B scenarios:
In Con-Reform seats, 50pc of Labour supporters will vote Tory to defeat Reform
Fewer than half of Reform supporters would vote Conservative to defeat Labour
Almost all Green supporters would vote Labour to defeat Reform
In Scottish seats between the SNP and Conservatives, Labour and Liberal voters are evenly split on which to support
In Welsh seats between Plaid and Reform, left-of-centre voters prefer to vote for Plaid
Overall, tactical voting benefits Labour and the Conservatives at the expense of almost everyone else. Reform loses ground mostly to the Conservatives, with maybe a little going to Labour.
The SNP also lose around 16 seats to tactical voting, but the other smaller parties are fairly stable.
Comparison with 2025
About a year ago, we conduced a similar 
tactical voting poll
.
Since then, anti-Reform sentiment has kept fairly strong. For example, 38pc of Labour supporters in 2025
said they would vote Conservative to stop Reform. This figure has risen to 50pc in this new poll.
On the other hand, some Restore voters would vote tactically to help Reform.
We can measure the size of these changes in seats, by running our MRP seat estimator
on the current voting intentions from our new poll, but using the tactical voting parameters
from respectively last year and this year.
Overall, the change in tactical voting preferences since 2025 increases Reform's seat total by
8 seats, while Labour loses 10 seats and the Conservatives gain 6 seats.

4. Electoral System – Party List PR
Proportional Representation aims to elect MPs so that a party's share of seats roughly matches its national vote share. For example, a party with 40pc of the vote should win around 40pc of the seats. This system makes single party majorities less likely and they usually result in coalition governments instead.
Because most parties have a real chance of winning in each constituency,
the need for tactical voting mostly disappears.
As an example Proportional Representation system we looked at the closed Party List system.
This is already used in Wales to elect the Welsh Senedd, and is also used in Scotland for Holyrood
elections in conjunction with FPTP.
It's not a highly democratic system, because parties have total control of the candidates
and of the order they appear on the party list. Candidates higher up the list are more likely to
be elected, and voters are not allowed to prefer one candidate over another. Voters only vote
for their single most favoured party.
We chose to have 130 big constituencies across the country, with each seat electing five members
of parliament. Members are elected using the
D'Hondt method
 of proportional representation.
The map shows the 130 big constituencies, with a 5-segment pie chart on each one showing the
breakdown of the elected MPs. For example, the seat in the north of Scotland has two SNP members (yellow),
and one each from the Conservatives (blue), Lib Dems (orange), and Reform (light blue).
As a check, we also ran the Single Transferable Vote (STV) system on the same constituencies and 
saw fairly similar answers (Con 124, Lab 168, Lib 58, Reform 161, Green 58, Restore 36, SNP 22, Plaid 7).
Overall, right-of-centre parties win more seats than left-of-centre parties, because 51pc
of people chose a right-of-centre party as their first preference, compared with 44pc for left-of-centre
parties.
5. Electoral System – Alternative Vote
The Alternative Vote (AV) is similar to First-Past-the-Post. Like FPTP, it elects one MP for each constituency, but instead of marking a single 'X', voters can rank the candidates in order of preference, putting a '1' by their favourite, a '2' by their second choice, and so on. Each voter can rank as many parties as they wish.
If a candidate gets more than half of the first-preference votes, they win. If not, the last-placed candidate is eliminated and their votes are passed on to the voters' next choices, and this repeats until someone passes 50pc. The UK voted on switching to AV in a referendum in 2011. The result was a firm 'No', with 68pc voting against and 32pc in favour.
By applying the voters' ranks to each seat, we can predict the winner under AV.
Reform are the big winners as they win more seats under AV than any other system.
The Conservatives are the main loser.
We can how this works by looking at the seat of Banbury in Oxfordshire. The Conservatives
are predicted to win this seat under FPTP, but they come third under the AV system.

6. Quotes
Martin Baxter, CEO of Electoral Calculus, said
: "Electoral reform is hugely popular as a policy on the left, but our data shows it would work against them. First-Past-The-Post with tactical voting safeguards left-of-centre majorities, and would allow Labour to form a majority with the Liberal Democrats if an election were held today. Switch to PR, and the picture transforms entirely, putting Nigel Farage in Downing Street alongside Kemi Badenoch and Rupert Lowe in senior cabinet positions.
"This creates a political tightrope for Andy Burnham. Touting proportional representation gives Labour a hook to unite the left, but actually delivering it could lock them out of power."
Tyron Surmon, Head of Research of Find Out Now, said
: "Britain now arguably has five major parties in a system only really designed for two. Against this backdrop, it is no surprise that the idea of electoral reform has again come into vogue. However, our polling shows that moving away from the current system could cost Labour over 100 seats and allow a right-wing government to take office. Whether this will dampen their enthusiasm for changing the voting system remains to be seen."

Find Out Now polled 10,684 GB adults online between 22 September to 12 October 2026. The sample was weighted to be representative by gender, age, social grade, other demographics and past voting patterns. Regression techniques were used to infer projected seat results.

Tactical voting in three-way races
We had to make assumptions about how voters would behave in three-way (or four-way)
marginals. In these cases the seat is not a straight two-horse race and simple tactical voting
models may be misleading. Our model assumptions are:
If the gap between the second and third placed parties is at least 10pc, then tactical voting 
	will operate in full. In this case the seat is a clear contest between two parties.
If the gap between the second and third placed parties is less than 5pc, then there will be
