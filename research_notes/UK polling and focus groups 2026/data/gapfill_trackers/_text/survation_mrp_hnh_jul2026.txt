First Past the Post, but only just: Reform’s marginal lead and our latest MRP findings
What Survation’s constituency MRP for HOPE not hate reveals about the next election’s tactical battleground
In July 2026, Survation carried out an MRP analysis of Westminster voting intention on behalf of HOPE not hate, based on a poll of 10,014 GB adults carried out between 22 and 27 July.
If an election had been held during our fieldwork, the result would have been a hung parliament: Reform the largest party on 227 seats and Labour on 197.  Reform, while the largest party, is still 99 seats short and it tops the projection on just 26.3% of the vote. What the model reveals is less clear-cut than the headlines might suggest, as we set out below. 
Reform leads most seats, but with slim margins across all
Reform’s lead is marginal – the median majority would be 7.6 points in seats Reform is likely to win, compared to a 18.9 points median majority for Labour in 2024. Just 100 of its seats would be safe, with a margin of 15 points or more, while 70 would be held by under 5 points. Across all 631 seats, 170 are decided by fewer than 5 points and 112 by fewer than 3. On these numbers, a national swing of just 6 points could flip around 95 seats. 
Tactical voting at the next election
Tactical voting was a defining feature of the 2024 General Election, as many voters of all stripes coordinated to remove Conservative incumbents. A shallow polling lead of 0.4% is exactly where tactical voting would become decisive for many seats, and there’s good reason to think the next election will see plenty of it. 
With voters across the spectrum currently dissatisfied with their traditional parties and with political preferences more fragmented than ever, the incentive to vote for whoever is best placed to block an unwanted winner only grows. The target on this occasion could be Reform UK, the party seen as the biggest threat across the board, or Labour which has alienated a significant proportion of its 2024 electorate. Under first-past-the-post, an anti-Reform tactical vote would mean a voter abandoning their preferred party when it is likely to finish third or lower to back the strongest challenger to Reform in their constituency. 
Recent by-elections bear out how decisive that can be: in Caerphilly as well as Gorton and Denton, a clear challenger emerged and the anti-Reform vote consolidated, behind Plaid Cymru and the Green Party respectively. Meanwhile, in contests where no obvious challenger crystallised, the anti-Reform vote split and Reform benefited, as we have observed in many local elections and by-elections over the past year. 
Tactical voting tends to materialise when voters have all relevant information: the national picture and the specific dynamics of their own seat. They need to understand who has the best chances of overtaking Reform and whether they can do so in their specific seat. MRP is useful here as a national poll can tell you Reform and Labour are level on around 26%., but can’t tell you whether, in your constituency, the challenger to Reform is Labour, the Liberal Democrats, the SNP or Plaid Cymru. Tactical voting is an inherently local question, and MRP, which models every constituency individually, helps answer that.
Where the votes came from and where Labour’s went
Reform’s vote is largely made up of recent converts. 39% of its current support also voted Reform in 2024, but a quarter did not vote at all, and 19% came from the 2024 Conservatives. 
Labour’s vote has the opposite makeup: they lose the most to abstention. Just under a fifth (18%) of Labour’s 2024 voters now say they will not vote, nearly twice the share going to Reform (10%) and almost three times the share going to the Greens (6%). Labour retains just 56% of its 2024 vote, which is the lowest of any major party, while Reform retains 78%.
The progressive split
Of the 227 seats Reform leads, Labour is second in 140, and in 134 of them the Green vote alone is larger than Reform’s winning margin. In many of these cases, Reform seems to be ahead only because the anti-Reform vote is divided between two or more challengers. 
One might think that Labour has lost votes to the Greens, but most Green voters were never Labour voters, so simply folding the Green vote into Labour likely won’t happen. When we model how the election could play out if every 2024 Labour voter who now backs the Greens returned to Labour, it moves 30 seats and makes Labour the largest party (with 225 seats to Reform’s 210) yet still far short of a majority. There are more seats where a united right would overturn the winner (164) than where a united left would overturn Reform (96). On current numbers, splitting is costing the right more than it is costing the left.

A country divided four ways
There are, however, different battles at play behind a national number:
The 
Red Wall
 – 160 post-industrial seats across the North, Midlands and Wales – is Reform’s surge zone: Labour’s vote is down around 12 points on average, Reform’s up nearly 15%, and it now leads in 101 of them. It is also where the “Burnham bounce” is largest, with Labour recovering roughly 11 points here since the spring.
Scotland and Wales
 are Labour’s weakest regions, but not because of Reform: here the vote is being reclaimed by the SNP in Scotland, who are on course for as many as 46 seats, and by Plaid Cymru in Wales. Labour’s retention falls to 45% in Scotland and 48% in Wales.
The 
Blue Wall
 – around 42 prosperous, graduate-heavy, Remain-voting seats in the South – remain the ideal ground for the Lib Dems. Labour’s vote is flat, the Liberal Democrats lead in 32, and Reform makes comparatively little headway.
London
 remains a Labour stronghold: the smallest Reform advance in the country at 6.5 points, the lowest Labour-to-Reform defection rate at 5%, and Labour still winning 52 of 75 seats.
The education divide
Education remains one of the strongest predictors of vote choice at both the individual and the constituency level. Reform’s share runs from 35% in the fifth of seats with the fewest graduates down to under 15% in the most graduate-heavy fifth. The correlation between a seat’s graduate share and its Reform vote is −0.78; against the 2016 Remain vote it is −0.86. The educational and Brexit fault lines of 2016 and 2019 are still structuring support for Reform in 2026. Meanwhile, the Conservative collapse is steepest in their traditional strongholds – seats with a higher share of the older population.
The Labour–Reform battleground
Most of the decisive contests are straight Labour–Reform fights: 259 of them in this polling, with Reform ahead in 140 and Labour in 119. Out of these contests, 79 are split by less than five points and 37 by less than two. In almost all of these seats our model puts each party’s win probability between 45% and 55% which is essentially a coin-flip based on current data.
An MRP doesn’t forecast the next election, and is a snapshot in time – in this case Andy Burnham’s first week as PM. Importantly, seat totals should always be read with their ranges, which can be wide. However it does show seat by seat races: which contests are close, who the most likely local challenger is, and where a divided anti-Reform vote is handing Reform seats it would not otherwise win. That map is the starting point for any serious analysis of whether, and where, tactical voting could reshape the result.
Survation conducted a multilevel regression and post-stratification (MRP) model of Westminster voting intention for HOPE not hate, based on 10,014 GB adults interviewed online between 22 and 27 July 2026, modelled to 631 constituencies.
