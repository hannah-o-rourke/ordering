## 2026-09-23 — 7. Reform UK Debate: Wales and the United Kingdom

*Speech* · [https://record.senedd.wales/Plenary/16263#C771730](https://record.senedd.wales/Plenary/16263#C771730)

Diolch, Llywydd. Just before I speak, I just wanted to echo what somebody else has said, and I really do think the First Minister should be in the Chamber for what I think is one of the most important debates that we'll have, together with some other Cabinet Members who are missing too.

So, when we debate Wales's place in the United Kingdom, we should do so with our eyes wide open about what separation would actually involve, because this isn't just about flags, slogans or constitutional theory. It is also about people's jobs, pensions, taxes and public services.

So, let's start with the numbers. Some of these have been mentioned already, but I want to go through them again with a little bit more detail. UK public sector debt now stands at almost £3 trillion, okay? There is no agreed formula determining how much of that debt Wales would inherit if it became independent, because that would still have to be negotiated. But just to understand the scale involved, if debt were divided according to population, Wales's share would be around £136 billion. That's approximately £43,000 for every man, woman and child here in Wales, and that's before we even consider Wales's annual finances.

The latest official figures show public expenditure attributed to Wales at around £20,000 per person, whilst revenue raised is only around £13,000. That gap of more than £7,000 per person equates to another figure that was mooted earlier, which is almost £23 billion every single year. Those figures don't tell us exactly what the finances of an independent Wales would look like, and nobody can honestly claim they do, but they do demonstrate the scale of the questions that would have to be answered. Who pays for the NHS? Who pays pensions? What taxes would have to rise? What spending would have to fall? And also, what currency would be used? Nobody seems to know. Who services that debt? And what happens to the fiscal transfers that currently operate across the United Kingdom? Those aren't scare stories; they're legitimate questions involving billions of pounds of taxpayers' money.

But our United Kingdom is about far more than just a balance sheet. Look at the history, some of which has been mentioned earlier, represented by families throughout Wales. Our fathers, grandfathers and great-grandfathers served alongside men and women from England, Scotland and Northern Ireland through two world wars. Welsh regiments fought with extraordinary courage, Welsh communities endured loss and sacrifice, and families waved loved ones goodbye, never knowing whether they would return. They served as part of the armed forces of the United Kingdom, and that shared history matters.

It doesn't dictate what constitutional decisions future generations must make—those decisions belong to us—but neither should we casually dismiss the bonds forged through generations of shared sacrifice, shared endeavour and shared achievements. Those bonds continue today. People cross the Wales and England border every day to work, trade, study, receive healthcare and visit their families. Businesses operate across it, supply chains cross it, families cross it. We share armed forces, we share economic institutions, we share risks. When one part of the United Kingdom needs support, resources can move between its nations and regions.

Wales can proudly and distinctively remain Welsh, whilst remaining part of something bigger too. Our language, our culture, our communities and our national identity do not depend upon erecting a new international border. When independence is proposed, the debate must go beyond the emotional appeal of greater sovereignty. Those advocating fundamental constitutional change need credible answers about debt, the fiscal deficit, pensions, currency, trade, defence and the funding of public services. The numbers involved are simply too large and the consequences too important for anything less. I believe every single one of us in this Chamber has a responsibility to understand the history and the legacy of the United Kingdom that generations before us helped us to build, defend and pass on.

We should think very carefully before taking divisive and potentially irreversible steps without confronting all of the consequences for those who come after us. For my part, I am proud to be Welsh, I am proud to be British, and I will continue to make the case for protecting our place within the United Kingdom, not only because of the history we share, but because of the future we still have the opportunity to build together. Diolch.
