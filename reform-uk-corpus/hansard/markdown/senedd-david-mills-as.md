## 2026-10-14 — 8. Short Debate: Green across the aisle: Making environmental stewardship a common cause

*Speech* · [https://record.senedd.wales/Plenary/16316#C779123](https://record.senedd.wales/Plenary/16316#C779123)

Environmental stewardship should not belong to one political party, one ideology, or one side of the Chamber. It should be a common cause. For small c conservatives, environmental conservation is central to our political philosophy. Yes, too often the tradition is overlooked because the debate about the environment has become almost entirely dominated by carbon reduction, which, whilst important, should not dominate Government action over other concerns. Many to the right, myself included, object to the direction of current environmental policy, but we should not allow that disagreement to be mistaken for indifference towards the environment itself. I believe climate change is a serious, potentially existential challenge that must be addressed, and we cannot simply ignore the evidence to pretend that a changing climate presents no risks, but we must distinguish between the challenge we face and the policies Governments choose to pursue in response to it.

I have profound concerns about the approach to net zero. The UK can impose ever more ambitious targets on itself and introduce costly regulations, but climate change is a global problem driven by global emissions. The effectiveness of our response must therefore be judged in a global context. My concern is that policies pursued in the name of net zero will make little difference to the global climate trajectory if they are not matched by meaningful international action, while not imposing enormous costs on ordinary people here at home, because those costs are not borne equally. We should not ask people on the lowest incomes to shoulder disproportionate costs for policies whose global impact depends on what the rest of the world does, when the UK's impact is so minuscule.

If the United Kingdom reached net zero by next week, would the climate stop changing? Of course it would not. That does not mean we should abandon efforts to reduce emissions, it means we should pursue them sensibly, in concert with other countries, and in a way that is economically sustainable and commands public support.

We should also be honest about the need to adapt to the changes in climate we face. We need resilient infrastructure, effective flood prevention, healthy soils, restored peatlands, and landscapes better equipped to withstand drought, wildfire and extreme rainfall. We must also consider the standardisation of air conditioning in homes and schools, and fund agricultural science that looks at how crops may have to adapt to a changing climate.

Mitigation and adaptation must go hand in hand, but my concern is that the single-minded focus of carbon targets has distracted us from other pressing environmental problems—problems where we could make tangible difference to people's lives and the natural world around us.

As we have been racing towards ever more ambitious carbon targets, our rivers and our seas have been filling with sewage. Natural Resources Wales reported that Dŵr Cymru was responsible for 155 pollution incidents in 2024. That was the highest number of sewage pollution incidents recorded by the company in a decade. We should be just as determined to stop sewage entering our rivers as we are to meet carbon targets.

Another area where we should be able to find common ground is the protection of our habitats and our beautiful landscapes. Species are disappearing, habitats are under pressure and too many landscapes are failing to reach their ecological potential. Tree planting, habitat restoration, healthier soils and protecting native species can all make a real difference, but this must be done in partnership with farmers and landowners, not through top-down diktats. We should reward long-term stewardship rather than simply measuring compliance with administrative requirements. We also need tougher penalties for environmental crime and better policing. While law-abiding citizens face increasingly costly waste disposal, criminals are dumping toxic waste and despoiling our countryside with apparent impunity.

Michael Sheen's recent documentary Buried has highlighted the illegal dumping of forever chemicals and acid sludge across England and Wales. In March, Natural Resources Wales reported that nearly 50,000 fly-tipping incidents had been recorded in Welsh local authorities in the previous year—an increase of almost 15 per cent. That's about 133 incidents every day. We need effective investigations and meaningful penalties. This is not about punishing well-meaning landowners for missing a clause in a regulation; it's about ensuring that those who wantonly destroy our natural environment face prison.

Presiding Officer, this brings me back to the central argument of this debate: environmental policy should not be judged solely on carbon targets, but by whether our rivers are cleaner, our habitats are recovering, our wildlife is thriving and our countryside is protected for future generations. Achieving this means working with farmers, landowners, conservationists, volunteers, communities and businesses. Good environmental policy need not be prohibitively expensive. Often, the best solutions are practical, locally led and rooted in an understanding of the landscapes. We can disagree about net zero and the pace of decarbonisation, but surely we can agree that sewage should not pollute our rivers, that endangered species should be protected, illegal dumping should be punished and those restoring habitats should be encouraged rather than obstructed. These aren't left-wing or right-wing objectives; they're commonsense objectives.

I'm confident the new Minister for climate change and rural resilience shares my desire to put sound land management and the protection of our natural inheritance at the heart of environmental policy, and I look forward to hearing how he intends to deliver that. Our responsibility as custodians is to ensure the next generation inherits a natural environment richer, healthier and better protected than the one we inherited. That's what environmental stewardship means to me. I look forward to hearing the other contributors to the debate. Thank you.
