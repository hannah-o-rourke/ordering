## 2026-09-23 — 7. Reform UK Debate: Wales and the United Kingdom

*Speech* · [https://record.senedd.wales/Plenary/16263#C771673](https://record.senedd.wales/Plenary/16263#C771673)

Addressing the motion before us, let me remind Members what the First Minister promised before the election. It was to establish a national commission on Wales's constitutional feature, not push for independence after 100 days. Yet after meeting the leaders of Sinn Féin and the SNP, this administration now appears to be charging full pelt towards independence. The joint Plaid, Sinn Féin and SNP agreement declares, and I quote,

‘Momentum is behind our movements; we believe that constitutional change is coming.’

The people of Wales deserve to know exactly what that means, exactly what it will cost, and exactly who will pay. Only last year, the First Minister declared that there was business to do right now. That meant, he stated, improvements in health, education, families, jobs, the environment and farming. And on that point, I agree. There is business to do, urgent business, serious business, and yet, instead of getting on with that, we seem to be heading down a constitutional rabbit hole.

Welsh families are worried about the Welsh NHS. They're worried about the cost of living. They're worried about whether their children will find jobs and build a future. And what is the answer from Plaid Cymru? More division, more distraction, more constitutional theatre.

After 100 days, this administration should be focused on what matters to the people of Wales. Patients are still waiting too long for treatment. Front-line services remain under pressure, and unemployment is too high. Energy bills for households and business are disastrous. Breaking the union will not fix any of this; it will make it worse.

And then there is the cost. Wales simply cannot afford the independence experiment. The Office for National Statistics demonstrated that the net fiscal deficit for Wales in 2024-25 was between £22 billion and £23 billion. And your own Minister, Dafydd Trystan Davies, Cabinet Minister for Government Effectiveness and the Constitution stated, and I quote,

‘it is plainly evident that Wales is a net fiscal beneficiary of the United Kingdom.’

What would be needed to pay for this? The answer: higher taxes and more borrowing. This is not a plan. It is a slow-motion fiscal car crash dressed up as a national project. And who pays? Who picks up the bill? The answer is simple: the Welsh taxpayer, Welsh families.

Leaving the United Kingdom would mean walking away from the benefits of a larger union, shared resources, fiscal transfers and economic scale. It's a gamble with people's jobs, mortgages, pensions—a gamble doomed to failure.

Then there is the plan to devolve the Crown Estate. This isn't the magic bullet Plaid claim. There is no Scottish-style windfall and no pot of gold at the end of the rainbow. The Welsh Government's own expert group said gross revenues would probably be less than £20 million a year—£20 million—and that is before the cost of creating another Welsh quango. It is obvious that all that Wales gains from being part of the United Kingdom is strength, financial security and shared opportunity. [Interruption.] No, sorry, can I just finish?

Being part of the union does not weaken Wales; it makes us stronger. The Senedd already has extensive powers over health, transport and education, so before Plaid Cymru put all their energy into breaking the union, they should answer a straightforward question: what have they done so far with the powers they already possess? What do the people of Wales actually want? Poll after poll after poll, the majority do not support independence. They want the Government to stop talking to itself and start listening to them. Welsh people want action that will revive the Welsh industry. Businesses, again, and energy-intensive industries in Wales are being squeezed by massive energy costs and bureaucracy—costs that prevent further investment, make them uncompetitive, and jobs are being lost. We need an energy policy built on affordability, reliability and national security. We need to scrap 'net stupid zero'. We should reassess the energy mix and support the roll-out of small modular nuclear reactors.

Wales's industry needs dependable power to be able to grow. The task will be impossible if we aren't part of the United Kingdom. The Welsh people want NHS waiting lists fixed. We need to clear the backlogs for all NHS treatments. The NHS needs more doctors, nurses and fewer managers. Welsh farming needs more support, not regulation. In Wales, we need farmers to produce more food. We should let farmers produce food, protect the countryside and sustain rural communities. Reform choose the United Kingdom. We need real action over pipe dreams and we need to keep Wales strong within the United Kingdom. Diolch.

## 2026-10-21 — 6. Members' Legislative Debate under Standing Order 11.21(iv): An NHS first Bill

*Speech* · [https://record.senedd.wales/Plenary/16318#C780727](https://record.senedd.wales/Plenary/16318#C780727)

I believe that the NHS first Bill has worthy ambitions, as long as the NHS is able to deliver the care when required. All my constituents ask for is a local medical centre or a hospital that can treat them quickly, with local expertise.

Gwyn Williams has often talked about how health board boundaries stop the movement of patients, staff and experts. This means that services are fragmented, duplicated and delayed. Patients should not be refused access simply because they live on the wrong side of a health board boundary. There must be no postcode lottery in treatment or outcomes. The ministerial advisory group recognised last year that de facto regional arrangements are already widely established and called for stronger regional working.

To support the Bill, there is clear need for more medical staff. I welcome the Government's announcement of 253 additional graduate nursing jobs recently, but the Royal College of Nursing has said that the uncertainty has caused a huge reduction in people accepting nursing degrees in Wales.

This Bill should also call for greater transparency. Health boards should publish clear information about the use of private providers, including costs, activities and outcomes. That would allow the public to judge whether spending leads to permanent NHS capacity or just short-term solutions. An NHS first approach must have stronger accountability. For too long, the Welsh Government has taken a hands-off approach to NHS management. Ministers must exercise firmer oversight and set clear expectations when performance, decision making or patient safety falls short. Successful practices should be shared across Wales. Our NHS needs management that is results driven, not process driven. We must allow our front-line health professionals to concentrate on what matters most: their patients.

What's needed is faster treatment, safer care and better use of public money. It is about building capacity and resilience within a national health service that belongs to us all. Let us put the NHS first, break down the barriers that delay care, strengthen democratic oversight and ensure that every decision begins and ends with what is best for the patients in Wales. Diolch yn fawr.
