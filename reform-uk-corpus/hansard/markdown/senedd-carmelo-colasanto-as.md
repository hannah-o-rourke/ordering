## 2026-07-01 — 7. Reform UK Debate: Nation of Sanctuary

*Speech* · [https://record.senedd.wales/Plenary/16082#C766056](https://record.senedd.wales/Plenary/16082#C766056)

I'm fully supporting this motion by Reform, and I'm going to give my rationale, as well as my own back story, to outline why I believe that. My parents were immigrants, I'm an immigrant. I was born in Baden, Aargau, Switzerland, in 1964. My parents left southern Italy and, along with thousands of Europeans, settled in Switzerland in the early 1960s as that Government sought to boost its economy and its infrastructure. My parents went as economic migrants, escaping the poverty of southern Italy at that time, in the hope of finding a home and being able to raise and start a family. All these people were admitted into the cantons of Switzerland, but were subject to rigorous checks and conditions based on where they were arriving from, as well as what skill sets they had and how they could contribute and integrate into those societies. Each family was issued with a family book, which carried the family's names and details, as well as a summary of the conditions of the Swiss Government for residents there.

Following that period and the influx into Wales by lots of Italians, my parents applied to come to Wales—again, as economic migrants—through the normal route. My father applied and was subsequently interviewed by the police and Home Office representatives and issued with an ID card in 1970, setting out the conditions for his and our stay in this country. He was required to carry this card at all times and produce it if and when requested by the police or any Home Office officials. My parents were also subject to further checks and vetting, which they both welcomed. It told them that this country that they were entering was a safe country that protected its people. Once here, my parents were not offered housing or any other benefits, no allowances or funds to assimilate into society, nor language classes—and nor were they expecting it. They had made a clear choice to seek a better life where they could contribute and call somewhere their home. My parents, especially my father, believed in making their own way in this world. He'd lost his father and mother when he was 14 years old. He believed that, if he had made a decision to come here, he was responsible, ultimately, for his own actions and to be able to support his own family.

Now, on to the salient point that I want to make: the nation of sanctuary project, whilst admirable in its ability to potentially identify and help people fleeing war zones, has somehow become a welcome banner for anyone wishing to come into the country. It is the very drain on our resources that my parents and others wanted to protect that is being used and being diverted from the most needy people and services in our society. Defenders of the nation of sanctuary will point to it as being small change. At the last count, it was £64 million, as if it's loose change that has suddenly been found down the back of the sofa. But every pound spent on an illegal immigrant who has come here simply to claim a better economic lifestyle, without contributing to the Welsh economy, is a pound that cannot be spent on the elderly, infirm, disadvantaged and homeless people of Wales. [Interruption.] I'm sorry, but respectfully, I'm going to carry on. It's quite a personal tale of my parents, so I'm just going to carry on here. Not to mention the services that require more funding, which are at breaking point.

I constantly hear the shouts of 'racist', which is nothing more than an intentional slur intended to close down a proper discussion around what my constituents and I are clearly saying is a major issue for them, as they struggle with the rising costs and failing services. And let's be very clear, I know what racism is. I didn't stand in a picket line, I didn't get a briefing on it, I didn't go to an online class. I suffered it. My parents suffered it. I watched as people racially abused my mother and father and mocked them because they couldn't speak proper English. We've got to move away from this. Mine is a lived experience, and I'm sure it was for other people in this Chamber. It was uncomfortable, sometimes very daunting, but my parents were always content and secure, and that was because they knew that all the vetting that they'd gone through wasn't simply to check their background and suitability. It was to allow them and others in this country the reassurance that we hadn't allowed people to be in this country who weren't meant to be here.

This policy banner of nation of sanctuary brings stress onto our economy that is not sustainable, as well as placing the people of Wales at risk from people who may—may—wish us harm, and criminals who have not been checked prior to resettlement in Wales. The nation of sanctuary has become the landing light that's been left on by the home owner. Unfortunately, by publicising it so strongly, the Welsh Government have also made the conscious decision to go downstairs and leave the front door open for everybody. The first obligation of any country is to make sure the welfare and the safety of its people are protected. I believe that, currently, this Government fails on that simple obligation. For that reason, I fully support the Reform motion today. Diolch.

## 2026-09-30 — 6. Reform UK Debate: Default 20 mph speed limit

*Speech* · [https://record.senedd.wales/Plenary/16265#C773807](https://record.senedd.wales/Plenary/16265#C773807)

Diolch, Lywydd. I'm going to cover a lot of ground that's already been covered, but there are two separate questions in this debate, and too often they're deliberately rolled into one. The first is whether 20 mph is right in some places. Well, of course it is; we're not going to argue with you over that. Outside schools, around hospitals, on clearly defined residential streets where the evidence justifies it, nobody on these benches disputes that lower speeds make collisions less severe. The second question is very different: should 20 mph be the default for restricted roads—I'm not going to use that term—across Wales, with communities left to argue their way back to 30 mph?

This is where Reform takes a different and pragmatic view. The focus should be on realising the data isn't conclusive—and it hasn't even been properly evaluated yet—and actually focus on that, rather than taking the credit for an initiative that was really poorly executed. Trying to take the credit for a small dip is like me taking credit for Italy winning the world cup in 1982 because I switched my tv on once. It's absolutely preposterous. It's delusional. For me, I'd like to give my own perspective on why we've seen this dip, and it has nothing to do with the B word. We're all getting e-mails, all of us, from constituents regarding speeding on specific roads, in parks at night. I have it in my ward with regard to the A484, which is a racetrack. I have it in North Dock in Llanelli, I have it in Llangennech in my ward. The most common factors, speaking to people and police officers for this behaviour and the change of behaviour, are as follows. Vehicles now have more safety measures than they ever did before—ever. There are fewer pedestrians around at night because they just don't go out because of the state of our roads. And these guys are speeding and driving at night. They do that because the police are spending less time patrolling in the evenings because of limited resources. So, there's another angle as to why you see that slight dip, and I think we're not looking at that.

There is a perception within people that lawlessness is acceptable, and the offenders that we should be focusing on take advantage of that. I'll give you an example of that perception. On 5 March 2026, I witnessed a car overtake me in Felinfoel in a 20 mph zone at approximately 60 mph. The car was going so fast that he actually had to swerve into oncoming traffic and narrowly missed a pedestrian by 1.5 ft to 2.5 ft. I phoned the police on 999 and was told to stay on the line whilst I followed the car in traffic. The registration number was given. Fifteen minutes later, I was told they couldn't do anything because he'd crossed into another force area. That was 5 March. To date, as of this morning, that person has not been visited and spoken to. This is a guy who almost killed someone. So, I would suggest that, actually, we're looking in the wrong place.

Now, Reform believes in far, far stronger enforcement of measures. Target the problem. Don't put a default in there. It's absolutely preposterous. So, back to the default. So, what currently decides whether you need your road to be a 20 mph zone? It isn't a school, hospital or a children's crossing. It's a lamppost because that's how we work our default out, by the space between the lights. So, if the street lights are close enough, it's 20 mph, and it's up to your community to prove otherwise, which is a nonsense.

Now, as I said, collisions on 20 mph and 30 mph roads are dipping slightly and every one of us should welcome that. But they were already falling for around 10 years. This isn't an overnight thing. The post-policy period is short and the Government's evaluation isn't even finished. So, I would say: publish the evaluation in full. Show us which roads actually produced a benefit, because if the gains have actually come from streets where 20 mph was always the default, then that's wrong, isn't it? That's an argument for targeting, not a national default.

Then there is consent: 469,571 people signed a petition to say they didn't want this—didn't want it. And we're accused of always talking Wales down, where it looks like they told you—500,000 people who didn't want it—and you talked them down. So, let's get our facts straight there. Transport for Wales's own monitoring sites in summer 2025 said that barely half of the vehicles are doing 24 mph or less. A default that half the drivers aren't keeping to has a credibility problem in itself. And when communities did push back, what happens? In Carmarthenshire, the council received 1,604 submissions from the general public. One hundred and fifty stretches of road were reviewed, and only 11 of those actually made it back to 30 mph.

Then there's the cost. The Government's own appraisal level predicted a net benefit. It estimated the cost of slower journeys at £6.35 billion over 30 years. And after counting every road safety, active travel and environmental benefit, its central estimate was still minus £4.5 billion.

Now to the Government. Plaid Cymru did not merely inherit this policy, you promised it and co-opted it. You promised it in your 2021 manifesto. You voted for it in 2022. Then, this May, you asked for a mandate to govern without a word about the default in your manifesto. At Reform, our manifesto is clear. Restore 30 mph as the default, keep 20 mph where it's generally needed outside schools and hospitals and on clearly defined residential streets, with a positive evidence-based case for each one. And invest in fixing dangerous junctions and collision black spots, instead of treating a change of signs as a road safety strategy and a cash cow that you can milk.

I urge Members to think today on the decision they make that will consign possibly one of our greatest regional clubs to the footnote of history. With that, I reject the amendment and support this motion fully.
