## 2026-09-23 — 7. Reform UK Debate: Wales and the United Kingdom

*Speech* · [https://record.senedd.wales/Plenary/16263#C771642](https://record.senedd.wales/Plenary/16263#C771642)

So, that is not an argument that Wales could not trade with England if it became independent, clearly it could, but it does demonstrate the sheer scale of the economic relationship that already exists in the United Kingdom. And that relationship operates with a common currency, a common monetary system and no exchange rate between Wales and England. We use sterling. Monetary policy is set through the Bank of England of the United Kingdom as a whole. A business, say, in Tenby buying goods from Birmingham does not have to think about currency conversions. A worker living in Powys and earning a salary across the border doesn't have to think about exchange rates. A pension does not change currency when somebody moves from Colwyn Bay to Chester. These things are easily taken for granted precisely because they happen every day.

There's also a pooling of resources. We hear often in this Chamber about fair funding, and there are legitimate arguments about the Barnett formula, individual spending decisions and how resources should be distributed, but that debate should also include the whole picture. The latest Office for National Statistics's country and regional public finance figures show that the public expenditure attributed to Wales substantially exceeds public revenues attributed to Wales. And the gap is phenomenal: in 2024-25 it was approximately £23 billion. Now, we should be precise about what this actually means. It's a measure of Wales within the present United Kingdom fiscal system, it's not automatically a prediction of the budget deficit of an independent Wales, but it should not be simply ignored.

One of the features of the United Kingdom is that revenue and expenditure are pooled across different parts of the country. At times, alternative areas contribute distinct amounts and have different spending needs. That pooling of resources is part of what a fiscal union actually means. The same principle can be seen in state pensions and much of social security. These remain UK-wide responsibilities under the current settlement. State pensions, pension credit, universal credit and disability benefit are all matters that basically are still reserved to the United Kingdom. Defence is shared. Foreign affairs are shared. Monetary policy is shared. There are therefore practical institutions behind the word 'union'. And none of this prevents Wales from having its own democratic institutions. The Senedd legislates in devolved areas, Welsh Ministers make decisions in those areas. The argument is not, therefore, a choice between having a Welsh voice and not having a Welsh voice. The present constitutional model combines Welsh self-government in devolved matters with participation in wider UK institutions.

Now, look, none of this means that the present system is perfect. No constitutional settlement is ever perfect. There will always be arguments about where power should lie, how money should be allocated and whether a particular decision should be taken in Cardiff or Westminster. We see it all the time. These are legitimate debates. But changing a constitutional settlement is entirely a different proposition. Plaid Cymru is clear about its ultimate aim. Its constitution states that it seeks independence for Wales in Europe. If independence is put forward as an alternative, it is reasonable to ask what would replace the arrangements I have just described. What currency would Wales use? Would it continue using sterling? If so, what influence would an independent Welsh Government have over monetary policy? Would a new Welsh currency eventually be created? What would happen to the fiscal arrangements that presently operate across the United Kingdom? How would pensions and social security liability be divided? What proportion of the UK assets and, crucially, liabilities would be negotiated? And what trading arrangements would apply if the independent Wales sought membership of the European Union while England remained outside it?

These are not reasons to avoid a constitutional debate, they are the substance of a constitutional debate. This is why I think we need to move beyond slogans on all sides. Those who advocate for constitutional change should explain what that change means in practical terms, and those who defend existing arrangements should be prepared to explain how they can work better. The people of Wales should be given the facts as opposed to simply competing assertions. For me, there are some straightforward questions that should apply whenever major constitutional change is proposed. What issue are we seeking to solve? What evidence is there that changing the constitution will solve it? What are the financial consequences? What happens to cross-border services? How will the new arrangements work, and who will be accountable for delivering them? These are sensible tests when a proposal concerns further devolution, independence or any other fundamental constitutional change.

And there is one final point: this debate should never become a test of somebody's Welshness. There are people deeply proud to be Welsh who support independence. There are people equally proud to be Welsh who support the union. Constitutional preference does not determine national identity.

So, Llywydd, as we consider this motion, and the amendments, I hope we can look beyond labels. We should examine the issues we already share: the economic relationship that already exists, the pooling of resources, the common currency, and the shared system of pension, social security, defence and international representation. Apologies for going over. Because whenever Wales ultimately chooses, decisions of this magnitude deserve to be made on the basis of evidence, practical detail and a clear understanding of the consequences. That is what I believe is the absolute basis for which this constitutional debate should take place. Thank you.

This issue is personal to me. My grandfather died from kidney failure caused by prostate cancer, and several other men on my mother's side of the family have been affected by it. I'm also thinking about the men who may have put off speaking to their GP. I'm 61 now, and because of my family background, I've had the prostate-specific antigen test, and, thankfully, the result was reassuring. But many men don't take that step. Some don't want to make a fuss. Some are worried about what they might be told. Others simply think if they feel fine, there cannot be anything wrong. The reality is that prostate cancer does not always give you a warning. That's why awareness matters and why men need to understand their own risk. But awareness on its own isn't enough. And inclusion in a strategy must mean more than just a mention in a document. It needs clear commitment on earlier diagnosis, timely treatment and fair access across Wales, with progress that patients and families can actually see—not because prostate cancer is more important than any other cancer, but because it affects so many men and families, and deserves sustained attention.

I'd like to thank organisations such as Prostate Cymru, Prostate Cancer UK, Lingen Davies Cancer Support in my own constituency, and Movember, along with the NHS staff and all the other organisations and individuals who work tirelessly to support patients and their families. Llywydd, I can't change what happened to my grandfather, but I'd like to think we can improve things for the men and families who come after us, because behind every diagnosis is a husband, father, grandfather, brother or friend. And what matters to those families isn't which party brought this debate forward; what matters is what we do next and whether we make the difference. Diolch.
